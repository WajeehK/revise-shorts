@intro Real circuits combine resistors, sources and sensors. This topic covers Kirchhoff's laws, combining resistances, internal resistance, and potential divider circuits used with sensors.
# Kirchhoff's laws
- **First law** (conservation of charge): the total current into a junction equals the total current out.
- **Second law** (conservation of energy): around any closed loop, the sum of the e.m.f.s equals the sum of the p.d.s.
!fig p-series-parallel Resistors in series and in parallel
:::eq
$$\text{series: } R=R_1+R_2+\dots\qquad\text{parallel: }\frac1R=\frac1{R_1}+\frac1{R_2}+\dots$$
:::
- In series, the current is the same everywhere and the p.d.s add up. In parallel, the p.d. is the same across each branch and the currents add up.
- A parallel combination always has a smaller resistance than its smallest branch.
:::ex Combined resistance
Q: A 4.0 Ω resistor is in series with a parallel pair of 6.0 Ω and 3.0 Ω. The supply is 12 V with no internal resistance. Find the current from the supply.
1. Parallel pair: 1/R = 1/6 + 1/3 = 1/2, so R = 2.0 Ω.
2. Total resistance = 4.0 + 2.0 = 6.0 Ω.
A: I = 12/6.0 = 2.0 A
:::
# Internal resistance
:::def
**Internal resistance (r):** the resistance of the source itself, which causes "lost volts" inside it.
**Terminal p.d. (V):** the p.d. across the terminals of the source when it delivers current.
:::
:::eq
$$\varepsilon=I(R+r)\qquad V=\varepsilon-Ir$$
:::
- As the current increases, the lost volts (Ir) increase and the terminal p.d. falls.
- A graph of V against I is a straight line: the **y-intercept is ε** and the **gradient is −r**.
:::prac PAG 4: determining e.m.f. and internal resistance
- Connect a cell to a variable resistor, with an ammeter in series and a voltmeter across the cell.
- Vary R and record V and I for each setting. Then plot V against I.
- Add a small fixed resistor in series to protect the cell, and do not leave the current flowing for long, because r changes as the cell warms up.
:::
:::ex Lost volts
Q: A cell with ε = 1.5 V and r = 0.50 Ω drives a current of 0.60 A. Find the terminal p.d. and the external resistance.
1. V = 1.5 − 0.60 × 0.50 = 1.2 V.
2. R = V/I = 1.2/0.60 = 2.0 Ω.
A: V = 1.2 V and R = 2.0 Ω
:::
# Potential dividers
!fig p-potdiv A potential divider with an LDR or thermistor
:::eq
$$V_\text{out}=\frac{R_2}{R_1+R_2}\times V_\text{in}\qquad\frac{V_1}{V_2}=\frac{R_1}{R_2}$$
:::
- Replace one resistor with an **LDR** or **thermistor** to make a sensing circuit. For example, with the thermistor as R₁ and V_out taken across a fixed R₂: as the temperature rises, the thermistor's resistance falls, so V_out rises.
- A **potentiometer** is a variable potential divider that gives a continuously variable output, from 0 to V_in.
:::ex Light sensor
Q: A 10 kΩ fixed resistor is in series with an LDR across a 9.0 V supply. V_out is taken across the LDR. The LDR is 2.0 kΩ in bright light and 50 kΩ in the dark. Find V_out in each case.
1. Bright: V_out = 2/(2 + 10) × 9.0 = 1.5 V.
2. Dark: V_out = 50/(50 + 10) × 9.0 = 7.5 V.
A: 1.5 V in bright light and 7.5 V in the dark. V_out rises as it gets darker, which could switch on a lamp.
:::
:::warn
- Forgetting the internal resistance when finding the current from a real cell.
- Adding parallel resistances directly.
- Getting the output the wrong way round. Check which resistor V_out is measured across.
:::
:::tip
- With several loops, write Kirchhoff's second law for each loop and solve the equations simultaneously.
:::
:::sum
- Kirchhoff's first law conserves charge; his second law conserves energy.
- Series: R = ΣR. Parallel: 1/R = Σ1/R.
- ε = I(R + r). On a V–I graph, the intercept is ε and the gradient is −r.
- Potential dividers with LDRs or thermistors make sensors.
:::
