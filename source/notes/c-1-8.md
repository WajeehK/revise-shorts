@intro Thermodynamics explains why ionic lattices form and whether a reaction is feasible at all. This topic covers Born–Haber cycles, lattice, hydration and solution enthalpies, perfect ionic model comparisons, entropy and Gibbs free energy.
# Enthalpy definitions for Born–Haber cycles
:::def
**Enthalpy of atomisation, ΔatH:** the enthalpy change when 1 mol of gaseous atoms is formed from the element in its standard state. Example: ½Cl₂(g) → Cl(g).
**First ionisation energy:** the enthalpy change to remove 1 mol of electrons from 1 mol of gaseous atoms. Example: Na(g) → Na⁺(g) + e⁻.
**First electron affinity:** the enthalpy change when 1 mol of gaseous atoms each gains an electron, forming 1 mol of gaseous 1− ions. Example: Cl(g) + e⁻ → Cl⁻(g). It is exothermic.
**Second electron affinity:** O⁻(g) + e⁻ → O²⁻(g). It is endothermic, because an electron is being added to a negative ion, which repels it.
**Lattice enthalpy of formation:** the enthalpy change when 1 mol of an ionic solid is formed from its gaseous ions (exothermic). **Lattice dissociation** is the reverse (endothermic, same magnitude).
**Enthalpy of hydration, ΔhydH:** the enthalpy change when 1 mol of gaseous ions dissolves in water to form 1 mol of aqueous ions (exothermic).
**Enthalpy of solution, ΔsolH:** the enthalpy change when 1 mol of an ionic solid dissolves in enough water that the ions are separated and do not interact.
:::
# Born–Haber cycles
A Born–Haber cycle uses Hess's law to find a lattice enthalpy, which can't be measured directly. Going round the cycle from the elements: ΔfH = ΔatH(metal) + IE(s) + ΔatH(non-metal) + EA(s) + LE(formation).
!fig c-born-haber Born–Haber cycle for sodium chloride
:::ex Lattice enthalpy of MgCl₂
Q: Use the following data (kJ mol⁻¹) to find the lattice enthalpy of formation of MgCl₂: ΔfH −642; ΔatH(Mg) +148; 1st IE +738; 2nd IE +1451; ΔatH(Cl) +121 (per mole of Cl atoms); EA(Cl) −349.
1. Add up the steps from the elements to the gaseous ions. Two Cl atoms are needed: 148 + 738 + 1451 + 2(121) + 2(−349) = +1881.
2. Then ΔfH = 1881 + LE, so LE = −642 − 1881.
A: LE(formation) = −2523 kJ mol⁻¹
:::
- **Lattice enthalpy becomes more exothermic** for **smaller ions** and **higher charges**, because the electrostatic attraction is stronger. For example, MgO is far more exothermic than NaCl.
- **Perfect ionic model comparison.** A theoretical lattice enthalpy assumes perfectly spherical ions with only ionic bonding. If the Born–Haber (experimental) value is **more exothermic** than the theoretical value, the compound has some **covalent character**. This happens when a small, highly charged cation polarises a large anion, as in AgI or MgI₂.
# Dissolving
$$\Delta_{sol}H=\Delta_{LE}H(\text{dissociation})+\sum\Delta_{hyd}H$$
- Hydration enthalpies are more exothermic for smaller ions with higher charges, because they attract the water molecules' dipoles more strongly. The δ− O faces cations; the δ+ H faces anions.
:::ex Enthalpy of solution
Q: Find ΔsolH of KCl. Lattice dissociation +711; ΔhydH K⁺ −322; ΔhydH Cl⁻ −364 kJ mol⁻¹.
1. ΔsolH = 711 + (−322) + (−364).
A: +25 kJ mol⁻¹ (endothermic, so the solution gets colder)
:::
# Entropy
:::def
**Entropy, S:** a measure of the number of ways that particles, and their energy, can be arranged. A higher S means more disorder. Units: J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.
:::
- S increases from solid to liquid to gas, and when the number of gas moles increases. For a perfect crystal at 0 K, S = 0.
$$\Delta S=\sum S(\text{products})-\sum S(\text{reactants})$$
# Gibbs free energy and feasibility
$$\Delta G=\Delta H-T\Delta S$$
- A reaction is **feasible** (thermodynamically possible) when **ΔG ≤ 0**.
- **Convert ΔS to kJ** (divide by 1000) before combining it with ΔH in kJ.
- The temperature at which a reaction becomes feasible is found by setting ΔG = 0: $T=\frac{\Delta H}{\Delta S}$.
- A graph of ΔG against T is a straight line with **gradient −ΔS** and **intercept ΔH**.
| ΔH | ΔS | Feasible? |
|---|---|---|
| − | + | at all temperatures |
| + | − | never |
| − | − | at low T (below ΔH/ΔS) |
| + | + | at high T (above ΔH/ΔS) |
!fig c-gibbs ΔG against temperature
:::ex Minimum temperature for feasibility
Q: CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g). ΔH = +178 kJ mol⁻¹, ΔS = +161 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. Find the lowest temperature at which it is feasible.
1. Convert: ΔS = 0.161 kJ K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.
2. Set ΔG = 0: T = 178 / 0.161.
A: T ≈ 1106 K (about 833 °C)
:::
- A feasible reaction may still not happen, because the **activation energy** can be so high that the rate is negligible. Diamond turning into graphite is an example.
:::warn
- Mixing J and kJ in ΔG = ΔH − TΔS.
- Forgetting to double the atomisation and electron affinity terms when there are two halide ions (as in MgCl₂).
- Mixing up the signs of formation and dissociation lattice enthalpies. State which one you mean.
- Using °C instead of K.
:::
:::tip
- Write every Born–Haber definition with state symbols and "1 mol". Examiners often ask for one definition plus an equation.
- When you explain lattice enthalpy trends, always mention ionic radius, ionic charge and the strength of electrostatic attraction.
:::
:::sum
- Born–Haber: ΔfH = the sum of atomisation, IE, EA and LE. Use it to find LE.
- LE is more exothermic for small, highly charged ions. An experimental value more exothermic than theoretical means covalent character.
- ΔsolH = LE(dissociation) + ΣΔhydH.
- ΔG = ΔH − TΔS. The reaction is feasible when ΔG ≤ 0, and T = ΔH/ΔS at the switch-over.
:::
