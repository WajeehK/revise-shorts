@intro Electric current is the flow of charged particles. This topic links what we measure in circuits (current and charge) to the motion of electrons inside a conductor.
# Charge and current
:::def
**Electric current (I):** the rate of flow of charge, $I=\Delta Q/\Delta t$. The unit is the ampere (A).
**Coulomb (C):** the charge that passes a point when a current of 1 A flows for 1 s. 1 C = 1 A s.
**Elementary charge:** e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. Charge is **quantised**: any charge is a whole-number multiple of e.
:::
- In metals the charge carriers are **electrons**; in electrolytes they are **ions**.
- **Conventional current** flows from + to −. Electrons flow the opposite way.
:::ex Counting electrons
Q: A current of 0.40 A flows for 2.0 minutes. How many electrons pass a point?
1. Q = It = 0.40 × 120 = 48 C.
2. N = Q/e = 48 / 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 3.0 × 10²⁰.
A: 3.0 × 10²⁰ electrons
:::
# Kirchhoff's first law
- **Kirchhoff's first law:** the sum of the currents entering a junction equals the sum of the currents leaving it.
- This is a consequence of **conservation of charge**: charge cannot build up at a junction.
:::ex Junction
Q: Currents of 2.0 A and 1.5 A enter a junction. One wire leaves carrying 0.5 A. Find the current in the other wire leaving.
1. Total in = 3.5 A.
2. Out: 0.5 + I = 3.5.
A: I = 3.0 A
:::
# Mean drift velocity
:::eq
$$I=Anev$$
:::
- A = cross-sectional area (m²), n = **number density** of charge carriers (m⁻³), e = charge on each carrier, and v = **mean drift velocity**.
- Electrons in a wire move randomly at high speeds, but their average drift along the wire is very slow, typically less than 1 mm s⁻¹. The signal (the electric field) travels at close to the speed of light, so a lamp lights almost instantly.
- In a wire that gets **thinner** while carrying the same current, v must increase, because A is smaller.
:::ex Drift velocity in copper
Q: A copper wire (n = 8.5 × 10²⁸ m⁻³) of area 1.0 × 10⁻⁶ m² carries 2.0 A. Find the drift velocity.
1. v = I/(Ane).
2. v = 2.0 / (1.0 × 10⁻⁶ × 8.5 × 10²⁸ × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹).
A: v = 1.5 × 10⁻⁴ m s⁻¹ (0.15 mm s⁻¹)
:::
- **Charge on ions** is a multiple of e too. For example, a Cu²⁺ ion carries +2e = 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁹ C.
# Conductors, semiconductors and insulators
| Type | Number density n | Example |
|---|---|---|
| conductor | very high (~10²⁸ m⁻³) | copper |
| semiconductor | much lower (~10¹⁷–10²³ m⁻³); increases with temperature | silicon |
| insulator | almost zero | plastic |
- For the same current, a semiconductor needs a much higher drift velocity, because n is much smaller.
- **Heating a semiconductor** frees more charge carriers, so n rises and the resistance falls. This is how a thermistor works.
:::warn
- Thinking electrons travel round a circuit quickly. The drift velocity is tiny.
- Forgetting to convert minutes to seconds in Q = It.
- Using the diameter as the area in I = Anev.
:::
:::tip
- Learn e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. It is in the data booklet, but you will use it constantly.
- When comparing two wires, write I = Anev for both and cancel the quantities that are equal.
:::
:::sum
- I = ΔQ/Δt; charge is quantised in units of e.
- Kirchhoff's first law (charge conservation): current in = current out at a junction.
- I = Anev. Conductors, semiconductors and insulators differ in n.
:::
