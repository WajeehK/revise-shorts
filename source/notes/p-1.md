@intro Module 1 is assessed through every written paper and the Practical Endorsement. It covers planning experiments, carrying them out safely, analysing data with uncertainties, and evaluating your conclusions.
# Planning
- **Independent variable:** the variable you change. **Dependent variable:** the variable you measure. **Control variables:** the variables you keep constant so the test is fair.
- Choose apparatus with a suitable **range** and **resolution**. For example, use a micrometer (0.01 mm) for wire diameter, but a metre rule (1 mm) for length.
- Include a **risk assessment**: identify the hazard, the risk and a control measure. Examples are goggles when wires under tension might snap, and a protective resistor to limit current.
- Decide how many readings to take (at least 5–6 values across the range), plus repeats, and how you will analyse them (usually with a straight-line graph).
# Implementing
- Record raw data in a table with **quantity / unit** headings, using a consistent number of decimal places that matches the instrument's resolution.
- Use appropriate techniques. For example, take readings at eye level to avoid **parallax**, check for **zero errors**, use **fiducial markers**, and time many oscillations instead of one.
# Analysing data
:::def
**Accuracy:** how close a measurement is to the true value.
**Precision:** how close repeated measurements are to each other (small spread).
**Random error:** unpredictable scatter about the mean. It is reduced by repeating and averaging.
**Systematic error:** a consistent shift in every reading, such as a zero error. Repeating does not reduce it.
**Uncertainty:** the interval within which the true value is expected to lie, e.g. 2.40 ± 0.05 V.
:::
:::eq
$$\text{absolute uncertainty of a mean}\approx\tfrac12(\text{max}-\text{min})$$
$$\%\text{ uncertainty}=\frac{\text{absolute uncertainty}}{\text{value}}\times100\%$$
:::
**Combining uncertainties:**
- When adding or subtracting quantities, **add the absolute uncertainties**.
- When multiplying or dividing, **add the percentage uncertainties**.
- When raising to a power $n$, **multiply the percentage uncertainty by $n$**.
:::ex Combining uncertainties
Q: A wire has diameter d = 0.50 ± 0.01 mm. Find the percentage uncertainty in its cross-sectional area, $A=\pi d^2/4$.
1. The percentage uncertainty in d is 0.01/0.50 × 100 = 2%.
2. A depends on d², so double it: 2 × 2% = 4%.
A: 4%. π and 4 are exact, so they add no uncertainty.
:::
# Graphs, gradients and error bars
!fig p-errorbars A best-fit line and a worst-fit line drawn through error bars
- Plot the independent variable on the x-axis. Choose scales that make the points fill more than half the grid, and label the axes with their units.
- Draw **error bars** for each point. The **best-fit line** passes through as many error bars as possible. The **worst-fit line** is the steepest or shallowest line that still goes through all the error bars.
:::eq
$$\%\text{ uncertainty in gradient}=\frac{|\,m_\text{best}-m_\text{worst}\,|}{m_\text{best}}\times100\%$$
:::
- Linearise relationships by comparing them with $y=mx+c$. For example, plot $T^2$ against $l$ for a pendulum. For power laws $y=kx^n$, plot $\lg y$ against $\lg x$: the gradient is $n$ and the intercept is $\lg k$.
:::ex Gradient uncertainty
Q: The best-fit gradient is 4.8 N m⁻¹ and the worst-fit gradient is 5.3 N m⁻¹. Give the result with its uncertainty.
1. The absolute uncertainty is |5.3 − 4.8| = 0.5 N m⁻¹.
2. The percentage uncertainty is 0.5/4.8 × 100 ≈ 10%.
A: k = 4.8 ± 0.5 N m⁻¹ (10%).
:::
# Evaluating
- Compare your result with the accepted value: percentage difference = |experimental − accepted| ÷ accepted × 100%. If the difference is smaller than the percentage uncertainty, the result agrees with the accepted value within experimental error.
- Identify the largest source of uncertainty and suggest a **specific improvement**. For example, use a longer length of wire, use light gates instead of a stopwatch, or use a data logger.

- 1 motion (e.g. finding g); 2 materials (the Young modulus); 3 electrical properties (I–V characteristics, resistivity); 4 circuits (internal resistance, potential dividers); 5 waves (diffraction gratings, stationary waves, the speed of sound); 6 quantum effects (the Planck constant using LEDs); 7 ideal gases (Boyle's law, absolute zero); 8 thermal properties (specific heat capacity, specific latent heat); 9 capacitors (charging and discharging); 10 SHM (pendulums, mass–spring systems); 11 nuclear decay (half-life models); 12 research skills.
:::
:::warn
- Saying that repeating readings reduces a systematic error. It does not; only a correction or recalibration does.
- Giving a final answer with too many significant figures. Match the data, usually to 2 or 3 s.f.
- Forcing a line through the origin when the data do not support it.
:::
:::tip
- "Precise" and "accurate" are not interchangeable. Use them exactly as defined above.
- When asked for an improvement, name the apparatus **and** explain how it reduces the uncertainty.
:::

- Plan with clear variables, suitable apparatus and a risk assessment.
- Uncertainties: add absolute values for sums and differences, add percentages for products and quotients, and multiply by n for powers.
- Error bars and worst-fit lines give the uncertainty in a gradient.
- Evaluate by comparing with accepted values and suggesting specific improvements.
