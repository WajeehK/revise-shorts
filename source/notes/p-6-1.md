@spec OCR H556 6.1.1 Capacitors; 6.1.2 Energy; 6.1.3 Charging and discharging capacitors (PAG 9)
@intro A capacitor stores charge, and therefore energy, on two conducting plates separated by an insulator. Its charging and discharging follow an exponential pattern that turns up all over physics.
# Capacitance
:::def
**Capacitance (C):** the charge stored per unit p.d., $C=Q/V$. 1 F = 1 C V⁻¹.
:::
- When a capacitor charges, electrons are pushed onto one plate and pulled off the other. The plates carry **equal and opposite** charges.
:::eq
$$\text{parallel: } C=C_1+C_2+\dots\qquad\text{series: }\frac1C=\frac1{C_1}+\frac1{C_2}+\dots$$
:::
- These are the **opposite** of the rules for resistors. In series, every capacitor carries the same charge Q.
:::ex Combining capacitors
Q: A 100 μF capacitor is in series with a parallel pair of 47 μF and 53 μF. Find the total capacitance.
1. Parallel pair: 47 + 53 = 100 μF.
2. Series: 1/C = 1/100 + 1/100, so C = 50 μF.
A: 50 μF
:::
# Energy stored
- The energy stored is the **area under a Q–V graph**, which is a straight line through the origin.
:::eq
$$W=\tfrac12QV=\tfrac12CV^2=\frac{Q^2}{2C}$$
:::
- Uses include camera flashes, back-up power supplies and defibrillators. Capacitors can deliver a large current quickly.
:::ex Defibrillator
Q: A 200 μF capacitor is charged to 2.0 kV. How much energy does it store?
1. W = ½CV² = ½ × 200 × 10⁻⁶ × (2000)².
A: W = 400 J
:::
# Discharging through a resistor
!fig p-cap-circuit Circuit for charging and discharging a capacitor
!fig p-discharge Exponential decay of charge during discharge
:::eq
$$Q=Q_0e^{-t/CR}\qquad V=V_0e^{-t/CR}\qquad I=I_0e^{-t/CR}\qquad\tau=CR$$
:::
- The **time constant**, τ = CR, is the time for Q, V or I to fall to 1/e (about 37%) of the initial value.
- The time to halve is t½ = 0.69CR (that is, CR ln 2).
- The decay is exponential because the rate of loss of charge is proportional to the charge remaining: dQ/dt = −Q/CR.
- Modelling: the change is ΔQ = −(Q/CR)Δt in each small step. A spreadsheet repeats this step to build the curve.
:::ex Discharge
Q: A 470 μF capacitor is charged to 12 V and discharges through a 10 kΩ resistor. Find V after 10 s.
1. τ = CR = 470 × 10⁻⁶ × 10 × 10³ = 4.7 s.
2. V = 12 e^(−10/4.7) = 12 × 0.119.
A: V = 1.4 V
:::
# Charging
- When the switch is closed, the current is largest at the start, because the capacitor has no p.d. to oppose the supply. As charge builds up, the capacitor's p.d. rises, the p.d. across the resistor falls, and the current decreases.
- After about 5CR, the capacitor is effectively fully charged to the supply p.d.
!fig p-charging Charging curves: V rises towards the supply while I falls
:::eq
$$V=V_0\left(1-e^{-t/CR}\right)\qquad Q=Q_0\left(1-e^{-t/CR}\right)$$
:::
- The current still decays exponentially during charging, $I=I_0e^{-t/CR}$.
:::ex Time to charge
Q: A 220 μF capacitor charges through a 4.7 kΩ resistor from a 6.0 V supply. How long does it take to reach 3.0 V?
1. τ = 220 × 10⁻⁶ × 4700 = 1.03 s.
2. 3.0 = 6.0(1 − e^(−t/τ)) gives e^(−t/τ) = 0.5, so t = τ ln 2.
A: t = 0.72 s
:::
:::prac PAG 9: finding the time constant
- Charge a capacitor, then discharge it through a known resistor. Record V every 10 s with a voltmeter or a data logger.
- Plot ln V against t. This gives a straight line with gradient −1/CR, so you can find C.
- Use a large CR, so that readings can be taken by hand. Repeat the run and average the results.
:::
:::warn
- Using the resistor combination rules for capacitors. They are the other way round.
- Forgetting the 1 − e⁻ form for charging.
- Leaving μF unconverted. 1 μF = 10⁻⁶ F.
:::
:::tip
- To find t from V, take logs: t = −CR ln(V/V₀).
:::
:::sum
- C = Q/V. In parallel C adds; in series 1/C adds.
- W = ½QV = ½CV².
- Discharge: x = x₀e^(−t/CR); τ = CR. A graph of ln V against t has gradient −1/CR.
:::
