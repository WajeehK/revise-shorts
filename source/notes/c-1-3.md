@spec AQA 7405 3.1.3.1–3.1.3.7
@intro The way particles are held together explains melting points, conductivity, solubility and shape. This topic covers ionic, covalent, dative and metallic bonding, the four crystal structures, VSEPR shapes, electronegativity and polarity, and the three types of intermolecular force.
# Ionic bonding
:::def
**Ionic bonding:** the electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions in a lattice.
:::
- Charges come from the Periodic Table. Group 1 forms 1+, Group 2 forms 2+, Al forms 3+, Group 6 forms 2−, and Group 7 forms 1−.
- Learn these compound ions: sulfate SO₄²⁻, hydroxide OH⁻, nitrate NO₃⁻, carbonate CO₃²⁻ and ammonium NH₄⁺.
- To write a formula, balance the charges. For example, Al³⁺ and SO₄²⁻ give Al₂(SO₄)₃.
# Covalent, dative and metallic bonding
:::def
**Covalent bond:** a shared pair of electrons. A double bond has two shared pairs; a triple bond has three.
**Co-ordinate (dative covalent) bond:** a shared pair of electrons where **both electrons come from the same atom**. It is drawn as an arrow from the donor atom.
**Metallic bonding:** the attraction between a lattice of positive metal ions and delocalised electrons.
:::
- Examples of dative bonds: NH₄⁺ (H⁺ accepts the lone pair from N), H₃O⁺, and Al₂Cl₆ (a Cl lone pair donated to Al). Once formed, a dative bond is identical to the other covalent bonds; all four N–H bonds in NH₄⁺ are the same.
- Metals with more delocalised electrons per atom and smaller ions (for example Mg compared with Na) have stronger metallic bonding and higher melting points.
# Crystal structures and properties
| Structure | Example | Bonding | Melting point | Conducts? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ionic | sodium chloride | ions in a lattice | high | only when molten or dissolved (ions free to move) |
| metallic | magnesium | positive ions + delocalised electrons | high | yes, solid and liquid (delocalised electrons) |
| macromolecular (giant covalent) | diamond, graphite | covalent network | very high | graphite yes, diamond no |
| simple molecular | iodine, ice | covalent within molecules, weak forces between molecules | low | no |
- **Diamond:** each C is bonded to 4 others in a tetrahedral network. It is very hard and has no free electrons.
- **Graphite:** each C is bonded to 3 others in flat layers. The fourth electron is delocalised, so graphite conducts along the layers. The layers are held by weak forces, so they slide.
- **Iodine and ice:** simple molecular lattices. Melting breaks only the intermolecular forces, not the covalent bonds.
- **Changes of state** need energy to overcome the forces between particles. The temperature stays constant during melting or boiling while that energy is absorbed.
# Shapes of molecules (VSEPR)
Electron pairs around the central atom repel each other and spread as far apart as possible. Lone pairs repel more strongly than bonding pairs: **lone pair–lone pair > lone pair–bond pair > bond pair–bond pair**. Each lone pair closes the bond angle by about 2.5°.
| Electron pairs (bonding + lone) | Shape | Angle | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 + 0 | linear | 180° | CO₂, BeCl₂ |
| 3 + 0 | trigonal planar | 120° | BF₃ |
| 4 + 0 | tetrahedral | 109.5° | CH₄, NH₄⁺ |
| 3 + 1 | trigonal pyramidal | 107° | NH₃ |
| 2 + 2 | bent (V-shaped) | 104.5° | H₂O |
| 5 + 0 | trigonal bipyramidal | 90° and 120° | PCl₅ |
| 6 + 0 | octahedral | 90° | SF₆ |
| 4 + 2 | square planar | 90° | XeF₄ |
!fig c-shapes Common molecular shapes and bond angles
:::ex Predicting a shape
Q: Predict the shape of, and bond angle in, PCl₄⁺.
1. P has 5 outer electrons. Lose 1 for the positive charge, giving 4. Each Cl contributes 1 electron to a bond, giving 4 bonding pairs in total.
2. There are 4 bonding pairs and 0 lone pairs.
A: Tetrahedral, 109.5°.
:::
:::ex A shape with lone pairs
Q: Predict the shape of ClF₃.
1. Cl has 7 outer electrons. Three of them pair with the 3 F atoms, giving 3 bonding pairs. The remaining 4 electrons form 2 lone pairs.
2. That is 5 electron pairs, based on a trigonal bipyramid. The two lone pairs sit in equatorial positions.
A: T-shaped, with bond angles slightly less than 90°.
:::
# Electronegativity and bond polarity
:::def
**Electronegativity:** the power of an atom to attract the pair of electrons in a covalent bond.
:::
- Electronegativity increases across a period and up a group. F is the most electronegative element, then O, N and Cl.
- Bonding electrons are shared unequally between atoms of different electronegativity. The result is a **polar bond** with partial charges, for example H$^{\delta+}$–Cl$^{\delta-}$.
- A molecule with polar bonds can still have **no overall dipole** if it is symmetrical, so the dipoles cancel. Examples are CO₂, BF₃ and CCl₄. CHCl₃ and H₂O are polar.
# Forces between molecules
1. **Induced dipole–dipole (van der Waals, London or dispersion) forces** act between all molecules. Electrons move at random, creating a temporary dipole that induces a dipole in a neighbouring molecule. These forces are stronger for molecules with **more electrons** (a larger $M_r$) and a **larger surface contact**, so straight chains have higher boiling points than branched ones.
2. **Permanent dipole–dipole forces** act between polar molecules, for example HCl or propanone.
3. **Hydrogen bonding** is the strongest of the three. It happens when H is bonded to **N, O or F**. The δ+ H is attracted to a lone pair on an N, O or F atom of another molecule. In a diagram, show the lone pair and the partial charges, with a bond angle of about 180° at the H.
!fig c-hbond Hydrogen bonding between water molecules
- **Anomalous boiling points.** H₂O, HF and NH₃ boil much higher than the trend in their groups predicts, because they form hydrogen bonds.
- **Ice is less dense than water.** Hydrogen bonds hold the molecules in an open lattice, so they are further apart than in the liquid.
:::ex Explaining boiling points
Q: Explain why ethanol (M_r 46) boils at 78 °C but propane (M_r 44) boils at −42 °C.
1. Their $M_r$ values are similar, so their van der Waals forces are similar.
2. Ethanol has an O–H group, so it forms hydrogen bonds between molecules. Propane has only weak van der Waals forces.
3. More energy is needed to overcome the hydrogen bonds.
A: Ethanol boils higher because of its hydrogen bonding between molecules.
:::
:::warn
- Saying that covalent bonds break when a simple molecular substance boils. Only the intermolecular forces are overcome.
- Saying that ionic solids conduct. They conduct only when molten or in solution, when the ions can move. It is the ions that carry the charge, not electrons.
- Drawing a hydrogen bond without the lone pair, or to an H that is not bonded to N, O or F.
- Forgetting lone pairs on the central atom when predicting shape.
:::
:::tip
- For a shape question, state the numbers of bonding pairs and lone pairs, then the shape and the angle. Then say that pairs repel to be as far apart as possible and that lone pairs repel more.
- For a melting-point question, name the structure, name the force or bond that has to be broken, and say how strong it is.
:::
:::sum
- Ionic bonding attracts oppositely charged ions; covalent bonding is a shared pair; in a dative bond both electrons come from one atom; metallic bonding is positive ions with delocalised electrons.
- The four structures are ionic, metallic, giant covalent and simple molecular. They explain melting points and conductivity.
- VSEPR: count bonding and lone pairs. Lone pairs repel most, removing about 2.5° per lone pair.
- Polarity comes from differences in electronegativity, but symmetrical molecules cancel out.
- The intermolecular forces in order of strength are induced dipole < permanent dipole < hydrogen bond.
:::
