@intro This topic goes inside the atom: the nucleus and the forces that hold it together, quarks and leptons, radioactive decay, and the energy released by fission and fusion.
# The nucleus
- **Alpha-particle scattering:** most alpha particles passed straight through gold foil, and a few were deflected through large angles. This showed that the atom has a tiny, dense, positive nucleus.
- A nucleus is written ᴬ_Z X, where A is the **nucleon number** and Z is the **proton number**. **Isotopes** have the same Z but different A.
- Nuclear radius: $R=r_0A^{1/3}$, with r₀ ≈ 1.2 fm. The density of nuclear matter is therefore roughly constant, at about 10¹⁷ kg m⁻³.
- The **strong nuclear force** is attractive up to about 3 fm and **repulsive below about 0.5 fm**. It acts between nucleons and holds the nucleus together against electrostatic repulsion.
# Fundamental particles
- Every particle has an **antiparticle**, with the same mass and the opposite charge. The positron is the antiparticle of the electron.
- **Hadrons** feel the strong force and are made of quarks. **Baryons** have 3 quarks (proton = uud; neutron = udd). **Mesons** are a quark plus an antiquark.
- **Leptons** (electron, muon, neutrino) do not feel the strong force and are fundamental.
- Quark charges: u = +⅔e, d = −⅓e, s = −⅓e.
- **Beta-minus decay:** n → p + e⁻ + ν̄ₑ (a d quark becomes a u quark). **Beta-plus decay:** p → n + e⁺ + νₑ (a u quark becomes a d quark). Both are caused by the **weak interaction**.
# Radioactivity
| Radiation | Nature | Ionising | Stopped by |
|---|---|---|---|
| α | helium nucleus | strongly | paper, or a few cm of air |
| β⁻ / β⁺ | electron / positron | moderately | a few mm of aluminium |
| γ | EM photon | weakly | several cm of lead (reduced, not fully stopped) |
- Decay is **random** (you cannot predict which nucleus will decay next) and **spontaneous** (it is not affected by external conditions).
:::eq
$$A=\lambda N\qquad N=N_0e^{-\lambda t}\qquad\lambda t_{1/2}=\ln2$$
:::
:::ex Activity
Q: A sample contains 2.0 × 10²⁰ nuclei with a half-life of 8.0 days. Find its activity, and the number of nuclei remaining after 24 days.
1. λ = 0.693 / (8.0 × 86 400) = 1.0 × 10⁻⁶ s⁻¹, so A = λN = 2.0 × 10¹⁴ Bq.
2. 24 days is 3 half-lives, so N = 2.0 × 10²⁰ / 8.
A: A = 2.0 × 10¹⁴ Bq, and 2.5 × 10¹⁹ nuclei remain.
:::
- **Carbon dating:** living things keep a constant ratio of ¹⁴C to ¹²C. After death the ¹⁴C decays (t½ ≈ 5700 years), so measuring the activity gives the age.
:::prac PAG 11: modelling decay
- Use dice or coins, or a protactinium generator, and correct each count for **background radiation**.
- Plot ln A against t. The gradient is −λ. Then find t½ = ln 2 / λ.
:::
# Mass defect, binding energy, fission and fusion
:::def
**Mass defect:** the difference between the total mass of the separate nucleons and the mass of the nucleus.
**Binding energy:** the minimum energy needed to separate a nucleus completely into its nucleons. It is given by Δmc².
:::
!fig p-binding Binding energy per nucleon against nucleon number
- Binding energy per nucleon peaks near **iron-56**. **Fission** of heavy nuclei and **fusion** of light nuclei both move towards the peak, so both release energy.
- **Fission reactors:** a thermal neutron is absorbed by uranium-235, which splits and releases 2–3 neutrons, giving a **chain reaction**. A **moderator** (water or graphite) slows the neutrons, **control rods** (boron) absorb neutrons, and **coolant** removes the heat. There are environmental issues with radioactive waste.
- **Fusion** needs very high temperatures, so the nuclei move fast enough to overcome their electrostatic repulsion. It powers stars.
:::ex Binding energy
Q: A helium-4 nucleus has a mass defect of 0.0304 u. Find its binding energy per nucleon (1 u = 931.5 MeV).
1. BE = 0.0304 × 931.5 = 28.3 MeV.
2. Per nucleon: 28.3 / 4.
A: 7.1 MeV per nucleon
:::
- **Quark model.** Hadrons are made of up (u), down (d) and strange (s) quarks and their respective anti-quarks (anti-up, anti-down and anti-strange). Charges: up +⅔e, down −⅓e, strange −⅓e; each anti-quark has the opposite charge. The relative sizes of the atom (about 10⁻¹⁰ m) and the nucleus (about 10⁻¹⁵ m) differ by a factor of about 10⁵.
- **Pair production and annihilation.** A photon of enough energy can cause the creation of a particle–antiparticle pair (for example an electron and a positron). When a particle and its antiparticle meet, annihilation converts their mass into two photons.
:::prac Absorption of radiation
Techniques and procedures used to investigate the absorption of α-particles, β-particles and γ-rays by appropriate materials: measure the background count, then the count rate with sheets of paper, aluminium of increasing thickness and lead between a sealed source and a GM tube. Alpha is stopped by paper, beta by a few mm of aluminium, and gamma is reduced by lead.
:::
- **Modelling decay.** Graphical methods and spreadsheet modelling of the equation ΔN/Δt = −λN: in each small time step, remove λNΔt nuclei. The resulting graph matches N = N₀e^(−λt).
- **Fission reactor.** The basic structure: fuel rods (enriched uranium), control rods (absorb neutrons, e.g. boron), a moderator (slows neutrons, e.g. graphite or water) and a coolant. These are its main components.
:::warn
- Saying that gamma rays are stopped completely by lead. They are attenuated, not fully stopped.
- Forgetting the neutrino or antineutrino in beta-decay equations.
- Using t½ in days with λ in s⁻¹. Keep the units consistent.
:::
:::tip
- In every nuclear equation, check that A, Z and charge balance. For particle reactions, check charge and baryon number too.
:::
:::sum
- Alpha scattering revealed the nucleus; R = r₀A^(1/3); the strong force holds nucleons together.
- Hadrons are made of quarks (baryons have 3, mesons have quark + antiquark); leptons are fundamental; beta decay changes the flavour of a quark.
- A = λN; N = N₀e^(−λt); λt½ = ln 2.
- Binding energy = Δmc²; fission and fusion both release energy; reactors use a moderator, control rods and coolant.
:::
