@intro Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy shows the different environments of hydrogen or carbon atoms in a molecule. It is the most powerful tool for working out an organic structure.
# How NMR works
- Nuclei such as ¹H and ¹³C behave like tiny magnets. In a strong magnetic field they absorb radio-frequency radiation at a frequency that depends on their **chemical environment**, meaning the atoms near them.
- The **chemical shift**, δ (in ppm), is measured relative to the standard **tetramethylsilane (TMS)**, Si(CH₃)₄, which is set at δ = 0.
- TMS is used because it gives **one strong sharp peak** (12 equivalent H atoms) upfield of most other signals, and it is inert, non-toxic and volatile, so it is easily removed.
- Samples are dissolved in solvents **without ¹H atoms**, such as **CDCl₃** (a deuterated solvent) or CCl₄, so the solvent does not give a signal.
# ¹³C NMR
- The **number of peaks** equals the number of different carbon environments.
- The chemical shift shows the type of carbon. For example, C=O is at 160–220 ppm, C–O at 50–90 ppm, and alkyl carbons at 5–40 ppm.
- Peaks are not split, and their heights are not used for counting atoms.
:::ex ¹³C environments
Q: How many peaks are there in the ¹³C spectrum of propan-2-ol, (CH₃)₂CHOH?
1. The two CH₃ carbons are equivalent, because of symmetry.
2. The CH carbon bonded to OH is different.
A: 2 peaks: one at about 25 ppm (CH₃) and one at about 64 ppm (C–O).
:::
# ¹H NMR
!fig c-nmr ¹H NMR spectrum showing splitting patterns
Three pieces of information come from a ¹H spectrum:
1. **Number of peaks:** the number of different H environments.
2. **Integration:** the relative peak areas, shown as an integration trace or a ratio, give the **relative number of H atoms** in each environment.
3. **Splitting (spin–spin coupling):** follows the **n + 1 rule**. A signal is split into n + 1 lines by **n H atoms on adjacent carbon atoms**.
| Neighbouring H atoms | Pattern |
|---|---|
| 0 | singlet |
| 1 | doublet |
| 2 | triplet |
| 3 | quartet |
- H atoms on O or N (in OH or NH) usually give a singlet and do not cause splitting.
- Use the data-booklet table of chemical shifts. For example, R–CH₃ is at about 0.9 ppm, CH₃–CO at 2.1 ppm, CH₂–O at 3.3–4.3 ppm, CHO at 9–10 ppm, and COOH at 10–12 ppm.
:::ex Predicting a ¹H spectrum
Q: Predict the ¹H NMR spectrum of ethyl ethanoate, CH₃COOCH₂CH₃.
1. There are three environments: CH₃CO, OCH₂ and CH₂CH₃.
2. The CH₃CO group has no adjacent H, so it gives a **singlet** with integration 3, at about 2.0 ppm.
3. The OCH₂ group is next to CH₃, so it gives a **quartet** with integration 2, at about 4.1 ppm.
4. The terminal CH₃ is next to CH₂, so it gives a **triplet** with integration 3, at about 1.3 ppm.
A: Three peaks, in the ratio 3 : 2 : 3 (singlet, quartet, triplet).
:::
:::ex Working out a structure
Q: A compound C₃H₆O gives a ¹³C spectrum with 2 peaks, one at 205 ppm. Its ¹H spectrum has a single peak at 2.1 ppm. Identify it.
1. The 205 ppm peak is a ketone or aldehyde C=O.
2. Only one ¹H environment means all six H atoms are equivalent, with no splitting.
3. Two CH₃ groups attached to C=O fit this.
A: Propanone, CH₃COCH₃
:::
:::warn
- Counting the H atoms **in** the group being split. The n in n + 1 refers to H atoms on the **adjacent** carbon.
- Forgetting symmetry, which makes some environments equivalent.
- Saying that TMS is used "because it is the solvent". It is the **reference standard**; the solvent is CDCl₃.
:::
:::tip
- For structure questions, set out a table: δ, splitting, integration, deduction. Then draw the final structure.
:::
:::sum
- δ is measured relative to TMS (0 ppm); samples use CDCl₃ or CCl₄ as the solvent.
- ¹³C NMR: the number of peaks gives the number of C environments; the shift gives the C type.
- ¹H NMR: peaks give environments, integration gives the H ratio, and splitting follows the n + 1 rule for adjacent H atoms.
:::
