@intro Acids and bases are proton donors and acceptors. This topic covers pH, the ionic product of water, strong and weak acids and bases, Ka and pKa, titration curves and indicators, and buffers, all with calculations.
# Brønsted–Lowry acids and bases
:::def
**Brønsted–Lowry acid:** a proton (H⁺) donor.
**Brønsted–Lowry base:** a proton acceptor.
**Strong acid:** completely dissociates in water, for example HCl, HNO₃ and H₂SO₄.
**Weak acid:** only partially dissociates, for example ethanoic acid. It sets up an equilibrium.
:::
- When an acid reacts with water, water acts as a base: HA + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + A⁻. Often this is simplified to HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻.
- Acid–base reactions involve transfer of a proton.
# pH and Kw
- [H⁺] in aqueous solutions ranges over many powers of ten (from about 1 to 10⁻¹⁴ mol dm⁻³), so a **logarithmic scale**, pH, is used. A change of 1 pH unit is a tenfold change in [H⁺].
$$\text{pH}=-\log_{10}[\text{H}^+]\qquad [\text{H}^+]=10^{-\text{pH}}$$
$$K_w=[\text{H}^+][\text{OH}^-]=1.00\times10^{-14}\text{ mol}^2\text{ dm}^{-6}\text{ at 298 K}$$
- In pure water [H⁺] = [OH⁻] = $\sqrt{K_w}$. Water is **neutral** at any temperature. Its pH is only 7 at 298 K: Kw increases with temperature, so the neutral pH falls.
- **Strong acid:** [H⁺] = concentration of acid (monoprotic). For example, 0.050 mol dm⁻³ HCl has pH = 1.30.
- **Strong base:** find [OH⁻], then [H⁺] = Kw/[OH⁻]. For example, 0.10 mol dm⁻³ NaOH: [H⁺] = 10⁻¹³, pH = 13.00.
# Weak acids: Ka and pKa
$$K_a=\frac{[\text{H}^+][\text{A}^-]}{[\text{HA}]}\qquad \text{p}K_a=-\log_{10}K_a$$
- For a weak acid on its own, assume [H⁺] = [A⁻] and that [HA] at equilibrium ≈ the starting concentration. Then $[\text{H}^+]=\sqrt{K_a\times[\text{HA}]}$.
- A larger Ka (smaller pKa) means a stronger acid.
:::ex pH of a weak acid
Q: Find the pH of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ propanoic acid, Ka = 1.35 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³.
1. $[\text{H}^+]^2=1.35\times10^{-5}\times0.100=1.35\times10^{-6}$.
2. $[\text{H}^+]=1.16\times10^{-3}$ mol dm⁻³.
3. pH $=-\log(1.16\times10^{-3})$.
A: pH = 2.93
:::
:::ex pH after mixing a strong acid and a strong base
Q: 25.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ HCl is mixed with 20.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ NaOH. Find the pH.
1. Moles of H⁺ = 2.50 × 10⁻³; moles of OH⁻ = 2.00 × 10⁻³. The excess H⁺ is 5.0 × 10⁻⁴ mol.
2. The total volume is 45.0 cm³, so [H⁺] = 5.0 × 10⁻⁴ / 0.0450 = 0.0111 mol dm⁻³.
A: pH = 1.95
:::
# Titration curves and indicators
!fig c-titration pH curves for strong and weak acids titrated with a strong base
| Acid + base | pH at equivalence | Suitable indicator |
|---|---|---|
| strong + strong | 7 | phenolphthalein or methyl orange |
| weak acid + strong base | above 7 (about 8–9) | phenolphthalein (range 8.2–10) |
| strong acid + weak base | below 7 (about 5) | methyl orange (range 3.1–4.4) |
| weak + weak | no sharp jump | none suitable |
- An indicator is suitable if its colour-change range lies completely within the **vertical section** of the curve.
- **Half-neutralisation.** At half the equivalence volume, [HA] = [A⁻], so **pH = pKa**. This is a quick way to find Ka from a curve.
:::prac Required practical 9: pH curves
- Calibrate the pH meter with buffer solutions first. Add the base in small portions, and in drops near the end point. Record the pH after each addition, then plot pH against volume.
- Read the equivalence volume at the middle of the vertical section, and pKa at half that volume.
:::
# Buffers
:::def
**Buffer:** a solution that resists changes in pH when small amounts of acid or base are added, or when it is diluted.
:::
- **Acidic buffer:** a weak acid and its salt, for example CH₃COOH and CH₃COONa. There is a large reservoir of both HA and A⁻.
  - Add H⁺: it reacts with A⁻ to form HA, so the equilibrium HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻ shifts left.
  - Add OH⁻: it reacts with H⁺ (or with HA) to form water. More HA dissociates, so the equilibrium shifts right.
  - In both cases the ratio [HA]/[A⁻] changes very little.
- **Basic buffer:** a weak base and its salt, for example NH₃ and NH₄Cl.
- **Uses:** shampoos, biological systems (blood is buffered at about pH 7.4 by H₂CO₃/HCO₃⁻), and calibrating pH meters.
$$[\text{H}^+]=K_a\times\frac{[\text{HA}]}{[\text{A}^-]}$$
:::ex pH of a buffer
Q: A buffer contains 0.20 mol dm⁻³ ethanoic acid and 0.10 mol dm⁻³ sodium ethanoate. Ka = 1.74 × 10⁻⁵. Find its pH, then the pH after 0.010 mol of HCl is added to 1.00 dm³ of it.
1. $[\text{H}^+]=1.74\times10^{-5}\times\frac{0.20}{0.10}=3.48\times10^{-5}$, so pH = 4.46.
2. Adding HCl turns A⁻ into HA. Now HA = 0.21 mol and A⁻ = 0.09 mol.
3. $[\text{H}^+]=1.74\times10^{-5}\times\frac{0.21}{0.09}=4.06\times10^{-5}$, so pH = 4.39.
A: pH 4.46, falling only to 4.39 when the acid is added.
:::
:::warn
- Using the weak-acid formula $\sqrt{K_a[\text{HA}]}$ for a buffer. Buffers contain added A⁻, so use $K_a\times\frac{[\text{HA}]}{[\text{A}^-]}$.
- Forgetting to divide by the **total** volume after mixing.
- Giving pH to the wrong precision. Use 2 decimal places.
:::
:::tip
- For buffer explanations, always write the equilibrium, say what the added H⁺ or OH⁻ reacts with, and say that the ratio [HA] : [A⁻] stays almost constant.
- For pH of diprotic acids such as H₂SO₄ at A-level, assume [H⁺] = 2 × concentration only if the question says so.
:::
:::sum
- Acids are proton donors. pH = −log[H⁺]. Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻] = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 298 K.
- Weak acid: Ka = [H⁺][A⁻]/[HA]. For the acid alone, [H⁺] = √(Ka[HA]).
- At half-neutralisation pH = pKa. Choose an indicator whose range lies in the steep part of the curve.
- Buffers resist pH change because they hold reservoirs of HA and A⁻. [H⁺] = Ka[HA]/[A⁻].
:::
