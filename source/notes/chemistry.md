## c-1-1
### Atomic structure
- Proton: relative mass 1, charge +1. Neutron: mass 1, charge 0. Electron: mass 1/1836, charge −1.
- Mass number $A$ = protons + neutrons. Isotopes have the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons, so they share chemical properties.
### Mass spectrometry (TOF)
- Stages: ionisation (electron impact, X → X⁺ + e⁻; or electrospray, X + H⁺ → XH⁺), acceleration, ion drift, detection.
- In electrospray the peak is at $M_r+1$.
$$KE=\tfrac12mv^2$$
$$t=d\sqrt{\frac{m}{2KE}}$$
$$A_r=\frac{\sum(\text{isotope mass}\times\text{abundance})}{\sum\text{abundance}}$$
!fig c-mass-spec
### Electron configuration
- Order: 1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d. Exceptions: Cr is [Ar]3d⁵4s¹ and Cu is [Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹. When transition metals form ions, the 4s electrons are lost first.
!fig c-orbitals
### Ionisation energy
- First IE: X(g) → X⁺(g) + e⁻. It depends on nuclear charge, distance from the nucleus, and shielding.
- Down a group, IE decreases. Across a period it increases, with dips at Group 3 (the electron is in a p orbital) and Group 6 (pair repulsion).
- Successive IEs show big jumps where a new shell is broken into, which gives the group.
!fig c-ie-period3
### Worked example
⁶⁹Ga 60.1%, ⁷¹Ga 39.9%: $A_r=\frac{69\times60.1+71\times39.9}{100}=69.8$.
### Exam tips
> Always include the state symbol (g) in IE equations.

## c-1-2
### The mole
$$n=\frac{m}{M_r}$$
$$n=c\times V(\text{dm}^3)$$
$$pV=nRT$$
- $N_A=6.022\times10^{23}\text{ mol}^{-1}$. For $pV=nRT$, use Pa, m³ and K (1 cm³ $=10^{-6}$ m³).
### Formulae and yields
- Empirical formula: divide % by $A_r$, then by the smallest, then scale to whole numbers.
$$\%\text{ yield}=\frac{\text{actual}}{\text{theoretical}}\times100$$
$$\%\text{ AE}=\frac{M_r(\text{desired})}{\sum M_r(\text{products})}\times100$$
- Water of crystallisation: heat to constant mass.
### Titrations
- Concordant titres agree within 0.10 cm³. Rinse the burette with the solution it will hold, and the pipette with the solution it will deliver.
### Worked example
25.0 cm³ of NaOH needs 22.50 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ H₂SO₄. Moles of acid $=2.25\times10^{-3}$, so NaOH $=4.50\times10^{-3}$ mol (2 : 1), and $[\text{NaOH}]=0.180\text{ mol dm}^{-3}$.
### Exam tips
> Use the mole ratio from the **balanced** equation, and convert cm³ to dm³ by dividing by 1000.

## c-1-3
### Bonding types
- **Ionic:** electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions in a giant lattice. High melting point; conducts when molten or dissolved.
- **Covalent:** a shared pair of electrons. **Dative:** both electrons come from one atom (e.g. NH₄⁺, H₃O⁺).
- **Metallic:** a lattice of positive ions in a sea of delocalised electrons.
- Giant covalent: diamond (4 bonds, hard), graphite (3 bonds, delocalised electrons, conducts, layers slide), SiO₂.
### Shapes (VSEPR)
- Pairs: 2 linear 180°; 3 trigonal planar 120°; 4 tetrahedral 109.5°; 5 trigonal bipyramidal 90°/120°; 6 octahedral 90°.
- Each lone pair reduces the angle by about 2.5°: NH₃ is 107° (pyramidal), H₂O is 104.5° (bent).
- Lone pairs repel more than bonding pairs.
!fig c-shapes
### Polarity and intermolecular forces
- Electronegativity: the power of an atom to attract the pair of electrons in a covalent bond (F is highest).
- Van der Waals (induced dipole) forces < permanent dipole–dipole forces < hydrogen bonds (N, O or F bonded to H, attracted to a lone pair on N, O or F).
- Ice is less dense than water because hydrogen bonds hold the molecules in an open lattice.
!fig c-hbond
### Exam tips
> A symmetrical molecule with polar bonds (e.g. CO₂, CCl₄) is **non-polar** because the dipoles cancel.
- When asked to explain a boiling point, name the force **and** say it is weak or strong relative to the others and needs more or less energy to overcome.

## c-1-4
### Enthalpy
- ΔH is the heat energy change at constant pressure. Exothermic: ΔH < 0. Standard conditions: 100 kPa and a stated temperature (usually 298 K).
- $\Delta_cH^\circ$: 1 mol burned completely in oxygen. $\Delta_fH^\circ$: 1 mol formed from its elements in their standard states.
!fig c-profiles
### Calorimetry
$$q=mc\Delta T$$
$$\Delta H=-\frac{q}{n}$$
- $m$ is the mass of water or solution (not the fuel). $c=4.18\text{ J g}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}$.
### Hess's law and bond enthalpies
- The enthalpy change is independent of the route taken.
$$\Delta_rH=\sum\Delta_fH_{\text{prod}}-\sum\Delta_fH_{\text{react}}$$
$$\Delta_rH=\sum\Delta_cH_{\text{react}}-\sum\Delta_cH_{\text{prod}}$$
$$\Delta H=\sum(\text{bonds broken})-\sum(\text{bonds made})$$
- Mean bond enthalpies are averages over many compounds, so results are less accurate.
!fig c-hess
### Worked example
50.0 g of water rises by 12.0 K when 0.0100 mol of fuel burns: $q=50.0\times4.18\times12.0=2508$ J, so $\Delta_cH=-251\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$.
### Exam tips
> Convert J to kJ and include the **sign**. Reasons a value is less exothermic than the data book: heat loss, incomplete combustion, non-standard conditions.

## c-1-5
### Collision theory
- To react, particles must collide with energy ≥ $E_a$ and with the correct orientation.
- **Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution:** the area under the curve is the total number of molecules. It starts at the origin and never touches the energy axis.
- Higher $T$: the peak moves right and lower, and many more molecules have $E\ge E_a$. This is the main reason the rate rises.
- Higher concentration or pressure: more frequent collisions.
- **Catalyst:** provides an alternative route with a lower $E_a$, and is not used up.
!fig c-maxwell
!fig c-catalyst
### Measuring rate
- Rate = change in concentration per unit time. Use the gradient of a tangent.
- Methods: gas volume (syringe), mass loss, colour change (colorimeter), clock reactions.
### Exam tips
> On M–B curves, the curve for a higher temperature has a **lower** peak, further to the right, with the same area.
- Don't say "particles collide more" for temperature. Say "more particles have energy above $E_a$".

## c-1-6
### Dynamic equilibrium
- Forward and reverse rates are equal, and concentrations stay constant (closed system).
- **Le Chatelier:** if a condition changes, the position of equilibrium shifts to oppose the change.
- Increasing pressure shifts to the side with fewer gas moles. Increasing $T$ shifts in the endothermic direction. A catalyst doesn't change the position, only how fast equilibrium is reached.
!fig c-equilibrium
### Kc
For $a\text{A}+b\text{B}\rightleftharpoons c\text{C}+d\text{D}$:
$$K_c=\frac{[\text{C}]^c[\text{D}]^d}{[\text{A}]^a[\text{B}]^b}$$
- Only **temperature** changes $K_c$. Units: cancel the concentration terms.
- Use an ICE table (initial, change, equilibrium). Divide moles by volume to get concentrations.
### Industrial compromises
- Haber process: 450 °C (rate vs yield), 200 atm (yield vs cost and safety), iron catalyst.
### Worked example
H₂ + I₂ ⇌ 2HI. Start with 1.0 mol of each in 1.0 dm³; 0.80 mol HI at equilibrium, so 0.60 mol each of H₂ and I₂ remain. $K_c=\frac{0.80^2}{0.60^2}=1.78$ (no units).
### Exam tips
> If the forward reaction is exothermic, increasing $T$ **decreases** $K_c$.

## c-1-7
### Redox
- **OIL RIG:** oxidation is loss of electrons; reduction is gain. An oxidising agent is reduced itself.
- Oxidation state rules: elements 0; O is −2 (−1 in peroxides); H is +1 (−1 in metal hydrides); F is −1; the sum equals the overall charge.
### Half-equations (acidic)
1. Balance the atoms other than O and H.
2. Add H₂O to balance O.
3. Add H⁺ to balance H.
4. Add e⁻ to balance the charge.
- E.g. MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5e⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O. Cr₂O₇²⁻ + 14H⁺ + 6e⁻ → 2Cr³⁺ + 7H₂O.
- To combine, multiply so the electrons cancel.
### Exam tips
> Name the oxidising agent as the **species**, e.g. "MnO₄⁻ ions", not just "Mn".
- Disproportionation: the same element is both oxidised and reduced (e.g. Cl₂ in water).

## c-1-8
### Born–Haber cycles
- Lattice enthalpy of formation: gaseous ions → 1 mol of solid lattice (exothermic). AQA also uses lattice dissociation, which is endothermic.
- Steps: atomisation, ionisation energy(s), electron affinity(s), lattice enthalpy, and $\Delta_fH$.
- The second electron affinity of O is **endothermic** (adding an electron to a negative ion).
- A more negative (exothermic) lattice formation enthalpy comes from smaller, more highly charged ions.
- Perfect ionic model vs experimental: a difference means **covalent character** (polarisation).
!fig c-born-haber
### Solutions
$$\Delta_{sol}H=\Delta_{LE,diss}H+\sum\Delta_{hyd}H$$
### Entropy and Gibbs
- Entropy $S$ is disorder: gas > liquid > solid. More moles of gas means $\Delta S>0$.
$$\Delta S=\sum S_{products}-\sum S_{reactants}$$
$$\Delta G=\Delta H-T\Delta S$$
- Feasible if $\Delta G\le0$. At the changeover $T=\frac{\Delta H}{\Delta S}$. Convert $\Delta S$ from J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ to kJ.
!fig c-gibbs
### Worked example
$\Delta H=+178$ kJ mol⁻¹ and $\Delta S=+161$ J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ (CaCO₃ decomposition): $T=\frac{178}{0.161}=1106$ K.
### Exam tips
> Feasible doesn't mean fast: a large $E_a$ can make a feasible reaction negligibly slow.

## c-1-9
### Rate equations
$$\text{rate}=k[\text{A}]^m[\text{B}]^n$$
- Orders are found by **experiment**, not from the equation. The overall order is $m+n$.
- Order 0: rate is independent of concentration. Order 1: rate ∝ concentration. Order 2: doubling the concentration gives 4× the rate.
- Concentration–time graphs: order 0 is a straight line; order 1 has a constant half-life. Rate–concentration graphs: order 0 is horizontal, order 1 is a straight line through the origin, order 2 is a curve.
- Units of $k$ come from rearranging, e.g. for a 2nd-order reaction: mol⁻¹ dm³ s⁻¹.
!fig c-rate-orders
!fig c-half-life
### Arrhenius
$$k=Ae^{-E_a/RT}$$
$$\ln k=\ln A-\frac{E_a}{R}\cdot\frac1T$$
- A plot of $\ln k$ against $\frac1T$ has gradient $-\frac{E_a}R$.
### Rate-determining step
- The slowest step. Species in the rate equation appear in (or before) the RDS.
### Worked example
A gradient of $-6000$ K gives $E_a=6000\times8.31=49.9\text{ kJ mol}^{-1}$.
### Exam tips
> Initial rates come from the tangent at $t=0$. A clock reaction gives rate ∝ $\frac1t$.

## c-1-10
### Kp
- Partial pressure $p_A=x_AP$, where the mole fraction is $x_A=\frac{n_A}{n_{total}}$.
For $a\text{A}(g)\rightleftharpoons b\text{B}(g)$:
$$K_p=\frac{(p_B)^b}{(p_A)^a}$$
- Only gases appear in $K_p$ (in heterogeneous equilibria, leave out the solids).
- Only temperature changes $K_p$. Changing the pressure moves the position, but $K_p$ stays the same.
### Worked example
N₂O₄ ⇌ 2NO₂ at 100 kPa, with mole fractions 0.6 and 0.4: $p_{NO_2}=40$ and $p_{N_2O_4}=60$, so $K_p=\frac{40^2}{60}=26.7$ kPa.
### Worked example 2 (from moles)
2SO₂ + O₂ ⇌ 2SO₃ at a total pressure of 200 kPa. At equilibrium there are 0.20 mol SO₂, 0.10 mol O₂ and 1.70 mol SO₃ (2.00 mol in total).
- Mole fractions are 0.10, 0.05 and 0.85, so the partial pressures are 20, 10 and 170 kPa.
$$K_p=\frac{170^2}{20^2\times10}=7.2\text{ kPa}^{-1}$$
### Effect of conditions
- If the forward reaction is exothermic, raising $T$ makes $K_p$ smaller, so the yield falls.
- Adding an inert gas at constant volume does not change the partial pressures, so the position does not move.
### Exam tips
> Check that the mole fractions sum to 1 and give the units of $K_p$ (kPa raised to some power, or none).

## c-1-11
### Electrode potentials
- Standard hydrogen electrode: H₂ at 100 kPa, 1.00 mol dm⁻³ H⁺, Pt, 298 K. E° = 0.00 V.
- Cell notation: oxidised species next to the salt bridge, e.g. Zn ∣ Zn²⁺ ∥ Cu²⁺ ∣ Cu.
$$E_{cell}^\circ=E_{right}^\circ-E_{left}^\circ=E_{red}^\circ-E_{ox}^\circ$$
- The more positive E° couple is reduced (it is the stronger oxidising agent). The reaction is feasible if $E_{cell}>0$.
- Salt bridge: filter paper soaked in KNO₃(aq), which lets ions move.
!fig c-she
### Cells
- Non-rechargeable: e.g. alkaline cells. Rechargeable: lithium-ion; the reactions reverse on charging.
- **Fuel cell:** H₂ + ½O₂ → H₂O. In alkali: anode H₂ + 2OH⁻ → 2H₂O + 2e⁻; cathode O₂ + 2H₂O + 4e⁻ → 4OH⁻. E ≈ 1.23 V. The only product is water, but H₂ needs energy to produce.
!fig c-cell
### Worked example
Zn²⁺/Zn −0.76 V and Ag⁺/Ag +0.80 V: $E_{cell}=0.80-(-0.76)=1.56$ V. Overall: Zn + 2Ag⁺ → Zn²⁺ + 2Ag.
### Exam tips
> Do **not** multiply E° values when you balance electrons.

## c-1-12
### Definitions
- Brønsted–Lowry acid: proton donor. Base: proton acceptor.
$$\text{pH}=-\log_{10}[\text{H}^+]$$
$$K_w=[\text{H}^+][\text{OH}^-]=1.0\times10^{-14}\ (298\text{ K})$$
- Pure water is always neutral: [H⁺] = [OH⁻], even when the pH is not 7 (e.g. at higher $T$).
### Weak acids
$$K_a=\frac{[\text{H}^+][\text{A}^-]}{[\text{HA}]}$$
$$[\text{H}^+]\approx\sqrt{K_ac}$$
$$pK_a=-\log K_a$$
- At half-neutralisation, pH = p$K_a$.
### Buffers
- A buffer resists pH change when small amounts of acid or base are added. Acidic buffer: a weak acid plus its salt (e.g. CH₃COOH / CH₃COONa).
$$[\text{H}^+]=K_a\frac{[\text{HA}]}{[\text{A}^-]}$$
- Blood is buffered by H₂CO₃ / HCO₃⁻.
### Titration curves
- Indicators: methyl orange (pH 3–4.5) for strong acid–weak base; phenolphthalein (pH 8–10) for weak acid–strong base. There is no sharp end-point for weak–weak titrations.
!fig c-titration
### Worked example
0.100 mol dm⁻³ ethanoic acid with $K_a=1.74\times10^{-5}$: $[\text{H}^+]=1.32\times10^{-3}$, so pH = 2.88.
### Exam tips
> For a strong base, find [OH⁻] first, then $[\text{H}^+]=\frac{K_w}{[\text{OH}^-]}$. For a diprotic strong acid, $[\text{H}^+]=2c$.

## c-2-1
### Periodicity
- Elements are arranged by proton number into s, p, d and f blocks. Period 3: Na and Mg are s-block; Al to Ar are p-block.
- **Atomic radius** decreases across a period: nuclear charge increases while shielding stays similar.
- **First IE** increases across a period, with dips at Al (3p electron, higher energy, more shielded) and S (pair repulsion in a 3p orbital).
### Melting points across Period 3
- Na < Mg < Al: metallic bonding gets stronger (more delocalised electrons, smaller ions with higher charge).
- Si is highest: giant covalent, so many strong covalent bonds must break.
- P₄, S₈, Cl₂, Ar: simple molecular, with weak van der Waals forces. S₈ > P₄ > Cl₂ > Ar because bigger molecules have more electrons.
### Exam tips
> Explain trends using **charge, distance and shielding** for IE and radius, and the **type and strength of bonding** for melting points.

## c-2-2
### Group 2 trends (down the group)
- Atomic radius increases, first IE decreases, and reactivity increases.
- Melting point generally decreases (larger ions, weaker metallic bonding).
- Hydroxide solubility **increases** (Mg(OH)₂ is sparingly soluble). Sulfate solubility **decreases** (BaSO₄ is insoluble).
### Reactions
- With water: M + 2H₂O → M(OH)₂ + H₂. Mg reacts very slowly with cold water but burns in steam: Mg + H₂O → MgO + H₂.
- Test for sulfate: acidified BaCl₂ gives a white BaSO₄ precipitate. HCl is added first to remove carbonate.
### Uses
- Mg(OH)₂: antacid. Ca(OH)₂: neutralising acidic soil. BaSO₄: barium meal (it's insoluble, so not toxic).
- CaO or CaCO₃: removing SO₂ from flue gases. Mg: extracting Ti from TiCl₄ (TiCl₄ + 2Mg → Ti + 2MgCl₂).
### Exam tips
> Barium compounds are toxic, but BaSO₄ is safe because it is insoluble. Say that explicitly.

## c-2-3
### Halogen trends
- Down the group: boiling point increases (more van der Waals), electronegativity decreases, and oxidising ability decreases.
- Appearance: F₂ pale yellow gas; Cl₂ pale green gas; Br₂ red-brown liquid; I₂ grey-black solid (purple vapour).
- Displacement: a more reactive halogen displaces a less reactive halide. Cl₂ + 2Br⁻ → 2Cl⁻ + Br₂ (orange).
### Halide ions as reducing agents
- Reducing power increases down the group (larger ions lose an electron more easily).
- With concentrated H₂SO₄: NaCl gives HCl only. NaBr gives HBr, Br₂ and SO₂. NaI gives HI, I₂, SO₂, S and H₂S (bad-egg smell).
### Silver halide test
- Add dilute HNO₃ (to remove CO₃²⁻ and OH⁻), then AgNO₃(aq). AgCl is white and dissolves in dilute NH₃. AgBr is cream and dissolves in concentrated NH₃. AgI is yellow and is insoluble in NH₃.
### Chlorine
- Cl₂ + H₂O ⇌ HCl + HClO is disproportionation; it is used to treat water (kills bacteria). In sunlight: 2Cl₂ + 2H₂O → 4HCl + O₂.
- Cl₂ + 2NaOH → NaCl + NaClO + H₂O makes bleach (cold, dilute alkali).
### Exam tips
> Explain the risk–benefit of chlorinating water: it's toxic, and can form chlorinated hydrocarbons, but it prevents disease.

## c-2-4
### Reactions with oxygen
- Na → Na₂O (yellow flame); Mg → MgO (white flame); Al → Al₂O₃; Si → SiO₂; P → P₄O₁₀ (white flame); S → SO₂ (blue flame).
### Oxides
- Na₂O, MgO and Al₂O₃ are giant ionic, with high melting points. SiO₂ is giant covalent, with a very high melting point. P₄O₁₀ and SO₂ are simple molecular, with low melting points.
- With water: Na₂O + H₂O → 2NaOH (pH 12–14). MgO gives pH about 9. Al₂O₃ and SiO₂ are insoluble. P₄O₁₀ + 6H₂O → 4H₃PO₄ (pH 0–1). SO₂ + H₂O → H₂SO₃; SO₃ + H₂O → H₂SO₄.
### Acid–base behaviour
- Basic oxides: Na₂O and MgO react with acids. Amphoteric: Al₂O₃ reacts with both acids and alkalis (Al₂O₃ + 2NaOH + 3H₂O → 2NaAl(OH)₄).
- Acidic oxides: SiO₂ (with hot concentrated NaOH, forming Na₂SiO₃), P₄O₁₀ (forming Na₃PO₄), SO₂ and SO₃.
### Exam tips
> Link the structure of each oxide to its melting point and its reaction with water, in the same answer.

## c-2-5
### What a transition metal is
- It forms at least one stable ion with a partially filled d sub-shell. Properties: complexes, coloured ions, variable oxidation states, catalysis.
### Complexes
- Ligand: donates a lone pair to form a coordinate (dative) bond. Monodentate: H₂O, NH₃, Cl⁻. Bidentate: en, C₂O₄²⁻. Multidentate: EDTA⁴⁻ (hexadentate).
- Shapes: octahedral (6 small ligands), tetrahedral (4 Cl⁻, e.g. [CuCl₄]²⁻), square planar (cisplatin), linear ([Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺).
- Isomerism: cis/trans (octahedral and square planar); optical (with 3 bidentate ligands).
- **Chelate effect:** multidentate ligands substitute monodentate ones because the number of particles increases, so ΔS > 0.
- Haem contains Fe²⁺. CO binds more strongly than O₂, which is why CO is toxic.
!fig c-complexes
### Colour
$$\Delta E=hf=\frac{hc}\lambda$$
- The d orbitals split. Electrons absorb visible light and are promoted; the complementary colour is seen. Colour depends on the metal, its oxidation state, the ligands and the coordination number.
- Colorimetry: measure absorbance against concentration with a calibration curve.
### Redox titrations and catalysis
- MnO₄⁻ (self-indicating, colourless → pale pink). MnO₄⁻ : Fe²⁺ = 1 : 5. Cr₂O₇²⁻ : Fe²⁺ = 1 : 6.
- Heterogeneous catalysis: V₂O₅ (Contact process), Fe (Haber). Homogeneous: Fe²⁺ for S₂O₈²⁻ + I⁻. Autocatalysis: Mn²⁺ in MnO₄⁻/C₂O₄²⁻.
### Exam tips
> Zn and Sc are d-block but **not** transition metals: Zn²⁺ is d¹⁰ and Sc³⁺ is d⁰.

## c-2-6
### Metal-aqua ions
- [M(H₂O)₆]²⁺ is weakly acidic. [M(H₂O)₆]³⁺ is more acidic because of its higher charge density, which polarises O–H more:
- [Fe(H₂O)₆]³⁺ + H₂O ⇌ [Fe(H₂O)₅(OH)]²⁺ + H₃O⁺.
### Precipitates
- Cu²⁺: blue solution; blue Cu(H₂O)₄(OH)₂ precipitate. In excess NH₃ it forms deep blue [Cu(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺.
- Fe²⁺: green precipitate, which goes brown in air. Fe³⁺: orange-brown precipitate.
- Al³⁺: white precipitate, which dissolves in excess NaOH to give [Al(OH)₄(H₂O)₂]⁻ (amphoteric).
- With Na₂CO₃: 2+ ions give MCO₃ precipitates; 3+ ions give M(OH)₃ precipitates **plus CO₂ bubbles**.
### Ligand substitution
- [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + 4Cl⁻ ⇌ [CuCl₄]²⁻ + 6H₂O (blue → yellow-green; octahedral → tetrahedral).
### Worked example
A solution of [Fe(H₂O)₆]³⁺ has a lower pH than one of [Fe(H₂O)₆]²⁺ of the same concentration. The 3+ ion has a higher charge density, so it polarises the O–H bonds in the water ligands more, releasing H⁺ more readily.
### Common mistakes
- Writing that NH₃ dissolves all hydroxide precipitates: of the ions in this topic, excess NH₃ dissolves only the copper hydroxide precipitate (forming [Cu(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺).
- Forgetting the CO₂ bubbles when 3+ ions react with carbonate.
### Exam tips
> Learn the colours as tables (ion × reagent × few drops/excess). They are easy marks.

## c-3-1
### Nomenclature and formulae
- Formula types: molecular, empirical, structural, displayed, skeletal. General formulae: alkanes CₙH₂ₙ₊₂, alkenes CₙH₂ₙ.
- Naming: find the longest chain, number from the end giving the lowest locants, and list substituents alphabetically (2-chloro-3-methylbutane).
### Isomerism
- **Structural isomers:** chain, position and functional-group isomers (e.g. propanal and propanone).
- **Stereoisomers:** E/Z isomerism needs restricted rotation about C=C and two different groups on each carbon.
- CIP rules: the atom with the higher atomic number has priority. Z means the priority groups are on the same side; E means opposite sides.
### Reaction types
- Homolytic fission gives radicals. Heterolytic fission gives ions.
- Curly arrows show the movement of an electron pair, from a bond or lone pair to an atom or bond.
### Exam tips
> Use skeletal formulae carefully: every corner and end of a line is a carbon, and H atoms on carbon are not shown.

## c-3-2
### Alkanes and fuels
- Saturated hydrocarbons. Fractional distillation of crude oil: there is a temperature gradient in the column; smaller molecules condense higher up.
- **Thermal cracking:** high $T$ and pressure, making many alkenes. **Catalytic cracking:** zeolite, about 450 °C, slight pressure, making aromatics and branched alkanes.
### Combustion and pollution
- Complete: CO₂ + H₂O. Incomplete: CO (toxic) and C (soot).
- NOₓ forms from N₂ + O₂ at high engine temperatures. SO₂ forms from sulfur impurities and causes acid rain; it is removed with CaO.
- Catalytic converter (Pt/Rh): 2CO + 2NO → 2CO₂ + N₂.
### Free-radical substitution
- Initiation: Cl₂ → 2Cl• (UV).
- Propagation: CH₄ + Cl• → •CH₃ + HCl, then •CH₃ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + Cl•.
- Termination: two radicals combine, e.g. •CH₃ + •CH₃ → C₂H₆.
- Ozone depletion: Cl• + O₃ → ClO• + O₂, then ClO• + O₃ → 2O₂ + Cl•. Cl• is regenerated, so it acts as a catalyst.
### Exam tips
> Further substitution gives a mixture of products, so free-radical substitution is a poor way to make one specific product.

## c-3-3
### Halogenoalkanes
- C–X is polar (C is δ+). Reactivity depends on **bond enthalpy**: C–I is the weakest, so it is hydrolysed fastest.
### Nucleophilic substitution
- OH⁻ (warm NaOH(aq), reflux) gives an alcohol. CN⁻ (KCN in ethanol/water) gives a nitrile, adding one carbon. NH₃ (excess, in ethanol, under pressure) gives a primary amine.
- Mechanism: the nucleophile's lone pair attacks the δ+ C, and the C–X bond breaks heterolytically, releasing X⁻.
!fig c-sn2
### Elimination
- KOH in **ethanol**, hot: OH⁻ acts as a **base**, removes H⁺ from the adjacent carbon, forms C=C, and X⁻ leaves.
- Tertiary halogenoalkanes favour elimination; primary ones favour substitution.
### Rate experiment
- Water + AgNO₃ in ethanol at the same temperature: time how long the precipitate takes to form (I fastest, then Br, then Cl).
### Exam tips
> In mechanisms, draw the lone pair on the nucleophile and the δ+/δ− dipole on C–X. Arrows start **at** the lone pair or bond.

## c-3-4
### Alkenes
- C=C is a σ bond plus a π bond. The π electrons are a region of high electron density, so alkenes attract electrophiles.
- Bromine water changes from orange to colourless.
### Electrophilic addition
- HBr gives a bromoalkane. Br₂ gives a dibromoalkane (Br₂ is polarised by the C=C). Cold concentrated H₂SO₄ gives an alkyl hydrogensulfate, which then forms an alcohol with water.
- Mechanism: π electrons attack the electrophile, forming a carbocation intermediate; then Br⁻ attacks.
- **Markovnikov:** the major product comes from the more stable carbocation (tertiary > secondary > primary). Alkyl groups have a positive inductive effect.
!fig c-elec-add
### Addition polymers
- Poly(ethene), poly(propene) and PVC are made from alkenes. Repeat unit: –CH₂–CH(CH₃)–.
- They are unreactive (strong non-polar C–C and C–H bonds), so they are not biodegradable. Plasticisers make PVC flexible.
### Exam tips
> For an unsymmetrical alkene, draw the carbocation and say **why** it is the more stable one.

## c-3-5
### Making ethanol
- **Hydration of ethene:** steam, H₃PO₄ catalyst, 300 °C, 60–70 atm. Fast and pure, but uses finite crude oil.
- **Fermentation:** C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂, with yeast at 35 °C and no oxygen. Renewable, but slow, and the product needs distilling.
- Bioethanol is called carbon-neutral, though in practice farming and transport release CO₂.
### Oxidation (acidified K₂Cr₂O₇, orange → green)
- Primary: aldehyde (distil it off as it forms), then carboxylic acid (reflux, excess oxidant).
- Secondary: ketone. Tertiary: not oxidised.
- Tests: Tollens' gives a silver mirror and Fehling's gives a brick-red precipitate with aldehydes, but not with ketones.
### Elimination
- Dehydration with concentrated H₃PO₄ or H₂SO₄ gives an alkene and water. This makes alkenes from renewable sources.
### Exam tips
> Use [O] in oxidation equations: CH₃CH₂OH + [O] → CH₃CHO + H₂O.

## c-3-6
### Tests for functional groups
- Alkene: bromine water decolourised. Aldehyde: Tollens' or Fehling's. Carboxylic acid: NaHCO₃ fizzes (CO₂).
- Primary or secondary alcohol: acidified dichromate turns green. Halogenoalkane: NaOH(aq), then HNO₃, then AgNO₃.
### Mass spectrometry
- The M⁺ peak gives $M_r$. High-resolution MS (to 4 d.p.) distinguishes compounds with the same integer $M_r$.
### Infrared spectroscopy
- Bonds absorb IR at characteristic wavenumbers: O–H (alcohol) 3230–3550 broad; O–H (acid) 2500–3000 very broad; C=O 1680–1750; C–H 2850–3300.
- Fingerprint region: below 1500 cm⁻¹, used to match a compound to a known spectrum.
- Greenhouse gases (CO₂, H₂O, CH₄) absorb IR, which contributes to global warming.
### Exam tips
> Quote the **bond** and the **range** from the data sheet when you identify an IR peak.

## c-3-7
### Key definitions
- **Stereoisomers:** same structural formula but a different arrangement of atoms in space. Optical isomerism is one type (E/Z is the other).
- **Chiral centre (asymmetric carbon):** a carbon atom bonded to **four different** atoms or groups. Mark it with an asterisk, C*.
- **Enantiomers (optical isomers):** a pair of molecules that are **non-superimposable mirror images** of each other.
- **Racemic mixture (racemate):** an equimolar (50 : 50) mixture of two enantiomers.
!fig c-enantiomers
### Plane-polarised light
- Each enantiomer rotates the plane of plane-polarised light by the same angle but in **opposite directions**.
- A racemic mixture is **optically inactive**: the two rotations cancel out.
- Enantiomers have identical physical properties (melting point, boiling point, density) and identical chemical reactions, except with other chiral molecules (e.g. enzymes and receptors in the body).
### Spotting chiral centres
- Check each sp³ carbon. A carbon with two H atoms (CH₂), or two identical groups such as two CH₃ groups, cannot be chiral.
- A C=C or C=O carbon is never a chiral centre (only three groups, planar).
- Look along the whole chain: in butan-2-ol, C2 carries H, OH, CH₃ and CH₂CH₃, which are all different, so it is chiral. Propan-2-ol (two CH₃ groups) is not.
### Forming racemic mixtures
- The C=O group is **planar**. In nucleophilic addition (e.g. KCN then acid with ethanal), CN⁻ can attack from **either face** with equal probability, giving equal amounts of both enantiomers.
- The same happens in electrophilic addition when a planar carbocation is attacked to make a new chiral centre, e.g. HBr + but-1-ene → racemic 2-bromobutane.
- A reaction that makes only one enantiomer (e.g. an enzyme-catalysed reaction) gives an optically active product.
### Worked example
Is 2-hydroxypropanoic acid (lactic acid), CH₃CH(OH)COOH, chiral?
C2 is bonded to H, OH, CH₃ and COOH: four different groups, so C2 is a chiral centre and the molecule has two enantiomers. C1 (COOH) and C3 (CH₃) are not chiral.
Draw the pair as a 3D tetrahedral C with one wedge and one dash bond, then reflect it in a mirror line, so that two groups swap sides.
### Drugs and synthesis
- Often only one enantiomer of a drug binds to the target receptor. The other may be inactive or harmful (the thalidomide tragedy).
- Synthetic routes through planar intermediates give racemates, which then need costly separation; chemists use chiral catalysts or enzymes to make a single enantiomer.
### Common mistakes
- Saying enantiomers have different boiling points or react differently with ordinary reagents: they do not.
- Drawing the "mirror image" as the same molecule rotated. Swap two groups or reflect it properly.
- Forgetting that a racemic mixture has **no** effect on plane-polarised light.
### Exam tips
> For "explain why the product is optically inactive", give three points: the C=O (or carbocation) is planar; attack is equally likely from above and below; a racemic mixture forms, whose rotations cancel.

## c-3-8
### Aldehydes and ketones
- Both contain C=O. Aldehydes: RCHO. Ketones: RCOR'.
- Aldehydes are oxidised (Tollens' gives a silver mirror; Fehling's turns from blue to a brick-red Cu₂O precipitate). Ketones are not.
### Nucleophilic addition
- NaBH₄ (reduction, [H]): aldehyde → primary alcohol; ketone → secondary alcohol. The nucleophile is H⁻.
- KCN, then dilute acid: gives a hydroxynitrile, adding one C. The nucleophile is CN⁻, and the product is racemic if chiral.
- Mechanism: the nucleophile attacks the δ+ C of C=O, the π bond breaks onto O⁻, then O⁻ is protonated.
!fig c-nuc-add
### Reduction and tests in detail
- Reduction with NaBH₄ in aqueous ethanol: ethanal + 2[H] → ethanol; propanone + 2[H] → propan-2-ol.
- Tollens' reagent ([Ag(NH₃)₂]⁺) is reduced to Ag; Fehling's (Cu²⁺, blue) is reduced to Cu₂O (red). The aldehyde is oxidised to a carboxylic acid (or its salt in alkali).
- Acidified K₂Cr₂O₇ also oxidises aldehydes (orange → green) but not ketones.
### Worked example
Ethanal + KCN, then acid: CN⁻ attacks the δ+ carbon, giving CH₃CH(OH)CN, 2-hydroxypropanenitrile. The C=O was planar, so attack from either face is equally likely and the product is a racemic mixture.
### Common mistakes
- Saying ketones give a positive Tollens' test: they do not.
- Drawing the curly arrow from the O instead of from the C=O bond, or from the nucleophile's negative charge instead of its lone pair.
### Exam tips
> KCN is used instead of HCN because HCN is a toxic gas, but KCN is toxic too. Work in a fume cupboard.

## c-3-9
### Carboxylic acids and esters
- Weak acids: RCOOH ⇌ RCOO⁻ + H⁺. With a carbonate they produce CO₂.
- Esterification: acid + alcohol ⇌ ester + water (concentrated H₂SO₄ catalyst). Esters are used in perfumes, flavourings, plasticisers and solvents.
- Hydrolysis: acid hydrolysis is reversible; alkaline hydrolysis goes to completion, giving a carboxylate salt.
- Fats and oils are triglycerides. NaOH hydrolysis gives soap plus glycerol. With methanol (KOH catalyst) they give biodiesel.
### Acyl chlorides and anhydrides
- Nucleophilic addition–elimination with water (forms an acid), an alcohol (ester), NH₃ (amide) or an amine (N-substituted amide). HCl is also formed.
- Anhydrides react the same way but more slowly and produce RCOOH instead of HCl. They are used industrially (e.g. for aspirin): cheaper and less corrosive.
- Mechanism: the lone pair attacks the δ+ C, the C=O π bond breaks to O⁻, then C=O reforms, Cl⁻ leaves, and H⁺ is lost.
### Exam tips
> Name esters with the alcohol part first: CH₃COOCH₂CH₃ is ethyl ethanoate.

## c-3-10
### Benzene
- C₆H₆. Planar, with all C–C bonds the same length (between single and double). A delocalised π ring.
- Evidence: ΔH of hydrogenation is −208 kJ mol⁻¹, not −360 (3 × −120), so benzene is about 152 kJ mol⁻¹ more stable than the Kekulé structure.
### Electrophilic substitution
- It keeps the stable delocalised ring, so benzene undergoes substitution, not addition.
- **Nitration:** concentrated HNO₃ + concentrated H₂SO₄, 50 °C. HNO₃ + 2H₂SO₄ → NO₂⁺ + H₃O⁺ + 2HSO₄⁻. Used for explosives and dyes (via amines).
- **Friedel–Crafts acylation:** RCOCl + AlCl₃ → RCO⁺ + AlCl₄⁻. Gives an aromatic ketone (e.g. phenylethanone); the catalyst is regenerated (H⁺ + AlCl₄⁻ → AlCl₃ + HCl).
- Mechanism: the ring attacks E⁺, forming a positive intermediate with a broken ring. C–H then breaks, restoring the delocalised ring.
!fig c-nitration
### Exam tips
> In the intermediate, draw a horseshoe (the partial ring) with + inside, open towards the sp³ carbon.

## c-3-11
### Amines
- Primary RNH₂, secondary R₂NH, tertiary R₃N. Quaternary ammonium salts R₄N⁺ X⁻ are cationic surfactants (fabric softeners and hair conditioners).
- They are bases: the lone pair on N accepts H⁺. Strength: primary aliphatic > NH₃ > phenylamine. Alkyl groups push electrons on to N; the benzene ring delocalises N's lone pair.
### Preparation
- From halogenoalkanes + excess NH₃. Further substitution gives a mixture of products.
- From nitriles: reduce with LiAlH₄ or H₂/Ni. This gives only the primary amine.
- Aromatic: nitrobenzene + Sn/concentrated HCl, then NaOH, gives phenylamine.
### Reactions
- As nucleophiles: with halogenoalkanes (substitution) and with acyl chlorides (addition–elimination, forming amides).
### Worked example
Rank in order of base strength: ethylamine > ammonia > phenylamine.
Ethylamine: the ethyl group is electron-releasing (positive inductive effect), so the N lone pair is more available to accept H⁺. Phenylamine: the N lone pair is delocalised into the benzene ring, so it is less available.
### More reactions
- Amine + acid → salt, e.g. CH₃NH₂ + HCl → CH₃NH₃⁺Cl⁻.
- Amine + acyl chloride → N-substituted amide + HCl (nucleophilic addition–elimination), e.g. CH₃COCl + CH₃NH₂ → CH₃CONHCH₃ + HCl.
### Common mistakes
- Explaining base strength with "electronegativity" instead of the availability of the lone pair on N.
- Forgetting that excess ammonia is needed to favour the primary amine.
### Exam tips
> Explain base strength by the **availability of the lone pair** on N.

## c-3-12
### Addition vs condensation polymers
- **Addition polymers** form from alkenes; the C=C opens and nothing else is produced. The chain is non-polar C–C bonds.
- **Condensation polymers** form when two different functional groups react, losing a small molecule (usually H₂O, or HCl if an acyl chloride is used) at every link.
- Condensation monomers need **two** reactive groups each (e.g. a diol, a dicarboxylic acid, a diamine), so the chain can grow at both ends.
!fig c-polymers
### Polyesters
- Diol + dicarboxylic acid → polyester + water; the link is the ester group –COO–.
- **Terylene (PET):** ethane-1,2-diol HOCH₂CH₂OH + benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid HOOC–C₆H₄–COOH. Used for drinks bottles and clothing fibres.
### Polyamides
- Diamine + dicarboxylic acid (or diacyl dichloride) → polyamide; the link is the amide group –CONH–.
- **Nylon-6,6:** hexane-1,6-diamine H₂N(CH₂)₆NH₂ + hexanedioic acid HOOC(CH₂)₄COOH. Each monomer has 6 carbon atoms, hence "6,6".
- **Kevlar:** benzene-1,4-diamine + benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid. Its flat, rigid chains line up and form many hydrogen bonds between N–H and C=O on neighbouring chains, so it is very strong (bullet-proof vests).
- Proteins are natural polyamides (polypeptides) made from amino acids.
### Working out monomers from a repeat unit
- Find each ester or amide link and break it: put –OH back on the C=O side, and –H back on the O or N side.
- With a diacyl dichloride monomer, the acid end is –COCl and HCl is lost instead of H₂O.
### Worked example
Repeat unit: –NH–(CH₂)₆–NH–CO–(CH₂)₄–CO–.
Break the amide links: the monomers are H₂N–(CH₂)₆–NH₂ (hexane-1,6-diamine) and HOOC–(CH₂)₄–COOH (hexanedioic acid). Each new link releases one H₂O, so a chain of $n$ repeat units releases about $2n$ water molecules (exactly $2n-1$ for a chain with free ends).
### Hydrolysis and disposal
- Ester and amide links are **polar**, so they are attacked by nucleophiles: they can be hydrolysed by acid or alkali and are **biodegradable**. Poly(alkenes) have only non-polar C–C and C–H bonds, so they are not.
- Acid hydrolysis gives the dicarboxylic acid plus the diol (or the diamine as its ammonium salt); alkaline hydrolysis gives the carboxylate salt plus the diol or diamine.
- **Landfill:** cheap but uses land, and the polymer persists for years.
- **Incineration:** releases energy that can generate electricity, but produces CO₂ and possibly toxic gases (e.g. HCl from PVC), which must be removed.
- **Recycling:** saves raw materials (crude oil) and energy, but plastics must be collected and sorted, which is costly.
### Common mistakes
- Leaving an –OH or –H on the end of a repeat unit. A repeat unit has open bonds (trailing lines) at both ends.
- Drawing the amide link backwards in the same chain; check that each link joins a C=O to an N–H.
- Calling poly(ethene) a condensation polymer. No small molecule is lost, so it is an addition polymer.
### Exam tips
> Draw the repeat unit with the link shown in full: C=O with the O or N–H attached, then brackets and a subscript n.

## c-3-13
### Amino acids
- H₂NCH(R)COOH. All except glycine are chiral. They exist as a zwitterion (H₃N⁺CH(R)COO⁻) at the isoelectric point. In acid they form the cation; in alkali, the anion.
### Proteins
- Peptide (amide) links are formed by condensation. Hydrolysis with 6 mol dm⁻³ HCl, under reflux, gives the amino acids back.
- Primary structure: the sequence of amino acids. Secondary: α-helix and β-pleated sheet (hydrogen bonds). Tertiary: 3D folding (hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, S–S bridges).
- Enzymes: stereospecific active sites. Drugs can act as inhibitors.
### DNA
- Nucleotide = phosphate + 2-deoxyribose + base (A, T, C, G). A–T has 2 hydrogen bonds; C–G has 3. The two strands form a double helix.
- Cisplatin binds to guanine on DNA, preventing replication. Side effects include harm to healthy cells.
### Exam tips
> Identify amino acids on a TLC plate using ninhydrin or UV, and compare their Rf values.

## c-3-14
### Organic synthesis
- Plan routes using the reaction map: alkane → halogenoalkane → alcohol → aldehyde or ketone → carboxylic acid → ester, amide, etc.
- To add a carbon: a halogenoalkane + KCN gives a nitrile. Then reduce it (amine), or use an aldehyde + HCN to make a hydroxynitrile.
- Key reagents: NaOH(aq) (substitution) vs KOH/ethanol (elimination); acidified K₂Cr₂O₇ (oxidation); NaBH₄ (reduction); concentrated H₂SO₄ (esterification, dehydration).
### Practical techniques
- Reflux, distillation, separating funnel (remove the aqueous layer), drying with anhydrous MgSO₄, recrystallisation (minimum hot solvent, cool, filter under reduced pressure), melting point to test purity.
- Overall yield of a multistep route = the product of the individual yields.
### Green chemistry
- Fewer steps, high atom economy, safer solvents, and catalysts.
### Exam tips
> For every step, give the **reagent, conditions and type of reaction**, and name the intermediate.

## c-3-15
### NMR spectroscopy
- TMS, Si(CH₃)₄, is the standard (δ = 0). It is inert, volatile and gives a single strong peak. Solvents with no ¹H, such as CDCl₃, are used.
- **¹³C NMR:** the number of peaks equals the number of carbon environments. Chemical shift identifies the type of carbon (C=O is about 160–220 ppm).
- **¹H NMR:** the number of peaks equals the number of H environments. The integration ratio gives the relative number of H in each environment.
- **Splitting (n + 1 rule):** a peak is split by n H atoms on **adjacent** carbons. A singlet has 0 neighbours, a doublet 1, a triplet 2, a quartet 3.
- O–H and N–H usually appear as singlets.
!fig c-nmr
### Worked example
Ethyl ethanoate CH₃COOCH₂CH₃ has 3 peaks: a singlet (3H, CH₃CO), a quartet (2H, OCH₂) and a triplet (3H, CH₂CH₃).
### Exam tips
> A quartet + triplet in a 2 : 3 ratio usually means an ethyl group.

## c-3-16
### Chromatography
- Separation depends on the balance between solubility in the **mobile phase** and retention by the **stationary phase**.
$$R_f=\frac{\text{distance moved by spot}}{\text{distance moved by solvent front}}$$
- **TLC:** silica or alumina on a plate. Locate spots with UV or iodine (or ninhydrin for amino acids). Compare Rf with known values under the same conditions.
- **Column chromatography:** a column packed with silica; the solvent (eluent) carries the components through at different rates. It is used to purify.
- **Gas chromatography:** the mobile phase is an unreactive gas. Retention time identifies a component and peak area gives its relative amount. It can be coupled to MS (GC–MS).
!fig c-tlc
### Practical tips
- Draw the start line in pencil, keep it above the solvent level, cover the tank with a lid, and mark the solvent front straight away.
### Exam tips
> Rf has no units and is always less than 1. If two spots have similar Rf values, rerun with a different solvent.
