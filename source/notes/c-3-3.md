@intro Halogenoalkanes contain a polar C–X bond, which makes them open to attack by nucleophiles and bases. This topic covers nucleophilic substitution, elimination, how the two compete, and how CFCs damage the ozone layer.
# The C–X bond
- Halogens are more electronegative than carbon, so the C–X bond is **polar**: C$^{\delta+}$–X$^{\delta-}$. The δ+ carbon is attacked by nucleophiles.
- **Reactivity depends on bond enthalpy**, not on polarity. C–I is the **weakest** bond, so iodoalkanes react fastest, even though the C–I bond is the least polar. The order of reactivity is C–I > C–Br > C–Cl > C–F.
- **Comparing rates of hydrolysis.** Warm each halogenoalkane with aqueous AgNO₃ in ethanol. Water acts as the nucleophile, and the halide ion released forms a precipitate. The yellow AgI precipitate appears first, then cream AgBr, then white AgCl.
# Nucleophilic substitution
A nucleophile (an electron-pair donor) replaces the halogen.
!fig c-sn2 Nucleophilic substitution: hydroxide ion attacks the δ+ carbon
| Nucleophile | Conditions | Product |
|---|---|---|
| OH⁻ | warm **aqueous** NaOH or KOH, under reflux | alcohol |
| CN⁻ | KCN in ethanol and water, warm | nitrile (the carbon chain gets one carbon longer) |
| NH₃ | **excess** concentrated ammonia in ethanol, heated under pressure | primary amine |
- Mechanism: the lone pair on the nucleophile attacks the δ+ C (curly arrow from the lone pair to C). The C–X bond breaks heterolytically (curly arrow from the bond to X), and X⁻ leaves.
- **With ammonia**, two molecules of NH₃ are used: the second removes H⁺ from the intermediate. For example, CH₃CH₂Br + 2NH₃ → CH₃CH₂NH₂ + NH₄Br. Excess NH₃ reduces further substitution to secondary and tertiary amines.
- **With cyanide**, bromoethane gives **propanenitrile**, CH₃CH₂CN.
# Elimination
- With **hot KOH in ethanol** (no water), OH⁻ acts as a **base**. It removes an H⁺ from the carbon next to the C–X carbon, forming an **alkene**: CH₃CHBrCH₃ + KOH → CH₂=CHCH₃ + KBr + H₂O.
- Mechanism: the OH⁻ lone pair attacks an H on the adjacent carbon. That C–H bond's electrons move to form the C=C, and the C–Br bond breaks to release Br⁻.
- Unsymmetrical halogenoalkanes can give **more than one alkene**, including E/Z isomers. For example, 2-bromobutane gives but-1-ene, E-but-2-ene and Z-but-2-ene.
# Substitution or elimination?
| Favours substitution | Favours elimination |
|---|---|
| aqueous solvent | ethanol solvent |
| warm | hot |
| primary halogenoalkanes | tertiary halogenoalkanes |
:::ex Predicting the product
Q: Name the organic product when 1-bromopropane is (a) warmed with aqueous NaOH and (b) heated with KOH in ethanol.
1. (a) Aqueous OH⁻ acts as a nucleophile and replaces Br, giving an alcohol.
2. (b) KOH in ethanol acts as a base and removes HBr, giving an alkene.
A: (a) propan-1-ol (b) propene
:::
:::ex Explaining relative rates
Q: Explain why 1-iodobutane is hydrolysed faster than 1-chlorobutane, even though the C–Cl bond is more polar.
1. The rate depends on how easily the C–X bond breaks.
2. The C–I bond enthalpy (about 228 kJ mol⁻¹) is much lower than that of C–Cl (about 346 kJ mol⁻¹).
A: The weaker C–I bond breaks more easily, so the reaction is faster. Bond enthalpy matters more than polarity.
:::
# Ozone depletion
- **Ozone** in the upper atmosphere absorbs harmful **UV** radiation: O₃ ⇌ O₂ + O. UV causes skin cancer and cataracts.
- **CFCs** were used in aerosols and refrigerants. In the upper atmosphere, UV breaks their C–Cl bonds to release Cl• radicals, which catalyse the breakdown of ozone:
$$\text{Cl}\bullet+\text{O}_3\rightarrow\text{ClO}\bullet+\text{O}_2$$
$$\text{ClO}\bullet+\text{O}_3\rightarrow\text{Cl}\bullet+2\text{O}_2$$
- Overall: 2O₃ → 3O₂. One Cl• radical can destroy thousands of ozone molecules because it is regenerated.
- NO• radicals from aircraft and lightning act in the same way.
- **Chemists' role.** Research confirmed the link between CFCs and the ozone hole, and legislation (the Montreal Protocol) banned CFCs. Chemists developed alternatives such as **HFCs**, which contain no chlorine.
:::warn
- Saying that C–F is the most reactive "because it is most polar". Bond **enthalpy** controls the rate.
- Using aqueous KOH for elimination. Use **ethanolic** KOH, heated.
- Forgetting that the Cl• radical is **regenerated**, which is why it acts as a catalyst.
:::
:::tip
- In mechanisms, show the δ+ and δ− charges, the lone pair on the nucleophile, both curly arrows, and the halide ion as a product.
- In elimination, the first curly arrow goes from OH⁻ to an H on the **adjacent** carbon, not to the C–X carbon.
:::
:::sum
- The C–X bond is polar, but bond enthalpy controls reactivity: iodoalkanes react fastest.
- Nucleophilic substitution with OH⁻(aq) gives an alcohol, with CN⁻ a nitrile, and with excess NH₃ an amine.
- Elimination with hot ethanolic KOH gives alkenes.
- Cl• from CFCs catalyses the breakdown of O₃. CFCs have been replaced by HFCs.
:::
