@spec OCR H556 5.2.1 Kinematics of circular motion; 5.2.2 Centripetal force
@intro An object moving in a circle at a constant speed is accelerating, because its direction keeps changing. A resultant force towards the centre, the centripetal force, causes this acceleration.
# Angular measure
:::def
**Radian:** the angle subtended at the centre of a circle by an arc equal in length to the radius. 2π rad = 360°.
**Angular velocity (ω):** the rate of change of angle, $\omega=\Delta\theta/\Delta t=2\pi/T=2\pi f$ (in rad s⁻¹).
:::
- The linear (tangential) speed is $v=\omega r$.
# Centripetal acceleration and force
!fig p-circular Velocity is along the tangent; the acceleration and force point towards the centre
:::eq
$$a=\frac{v^2}{r}=\omega^2r\qquad F=\frac{mv^2}{r}=m\omega^2r$$
:::
- The velocity is **tangential**, and the acceleration is **towards the centre**. The force does **no work**, because it is perpendicular to the motion, so the speed stays constant.
- The centripetal force is not a new kind of force. It is the resultant of real forces: tension for a stone on a string, friction for a car on a bend, gravity for an orbiting satellite.
:::ex Car on a bend
Q: A 1200 kg car travels round a bend of radius 50 m at 15 m s⁻¹. What friction force is needed?
1. F = mv²/r = 1200 × 225 / 50.
A: F = 5400 N, provided by friction between the tyres and the road, acting towards the centre.
:::
:::ex Angular velocity
Q: A wheel turns at 600 revolutions per minute. Find ω, and the speed of a point 0.30 m from the axle.
1. f = 600/60 = 10 Hz, so ω = 2π × 10 = 62.8 rad s⁻¹.
2. v = ωr = 62.8 × 0.30 = 18.8 m s⁻¹.
A: ω = 63 rad s⁻¹ and v = 19 m s⁻¹
:::
# Why a centripetal force is needed
- By Newton's first law, without a resultant force an object would carry on in a straight line along the tangent. A resultant force perpendicular to the velocity keeps changing the **direction** but not the speed.
- If the force is removed (for example, the string breaks), the object flies off **along the tangent**, not outwards along the radius.
# Vertical circles
- At the **top** of a vertical circle (for a mass on a string): T + mg = mv²/r. At the **bottom**: T − mg = mv²/r, so the tension is greatest at the bottom.
- The minimum speed at the top occurs when T = 0: $v=\sqrt{gr}$.
:::ex Bucket of water
Q: A bucket is swung in a vertical circle of radius 0.80 m. What is the minimum speed at the top so that the water stays in?
1. At the minimum speed, gravity alone provides the centripetal force: mg = mv²/r.
2. v = √(9.81 × 0.80) = 2.8 m s⁻¹.
A: 2.8 m s⁻¹
:::
:::ex Tension at the bottom
Q: A 0.50 kg mass moves at 4.0 m s⁻¹ at the bottom of a vertical circle of radius 0.60 m. Find the tension in the string.
1. T − mg = mv²/r, so T = 0.50 × 9.81 + 0.50 × 16 / 0.60.
2. T = 4.9 + 13.3 = 18.2 N.
A: About 18 N, far more than the weight of 4.9 N.
:::
:::prac Investigating F = mv²/r
- Whirl a rubber bung on a string through a glass tube, with a hanging mass providing the tension. Measure the radius and time 10 revolutions.
- Then vary the hanging mass and check that F ∝ v² at a fixed radius. Wear goggles and keep the area clear.
:::
:::warn
- Drawing a "centrifugal force" outwards on a free-body diagram. In an inertial frame there is no such force.
- Using degrees instead of radians in ω calculations.
- Saying that the speed changes in uniform circular motion. It is the **velocity** that changes.
:::
:::tip
- Identify which real force provides the centripetal force before writing F = mv²/r.
:::
:::sum
- ω = 2π/T and v = ωr.
- a = v²/r = ω²r, directed towards the centre; F = mv²/r.
- The centripetal force is the resultant of real forces and does no work.
:::
