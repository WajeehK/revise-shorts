@intro Kinetics is about how fast reactions go. This topic covers collision theory and activation energy, the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution, and how temperature, concentration, pressure and catalysts change the rate.
# Collision theory and rate
:::def
**Rate of reaction:** the change in concentration (or amount) of a reactant or product per unit time, for example in mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹.
**Activation energy, Eₐ:** the minimum energy that particles need to collide with for a reaction to happen.
:::
- For particles to react they must **collide**, with the **correct orientation** and with energy **at least Eₐ**.
- Most collisions do not lead to a reaction, because most particles have less energy than Eₐ or collide the wrong way round.
- On a concentration–time graph, the rate at any time is the **gradient of the tangent**. The initial rate is the gradient at $t=0$.
# The Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution
!fig c-maxwell Maxwell–Boltzmann distributions at two temperatures
- The curve shows how many molecules in a gas have each energy.
- It **starts at the origin**, because no molecules have zero energy.
- It has **no maximum energy**: the curve approaches the energy axis but never touches it.
- The **peak** is the most probable energy. The mean energy is a little to the right of the peak.
- The **area under the curve** is the total number of molecules.
- Molecules to the right of Eₐ have enough energy to react.
# Effect of temperature
- At a higher temperature the peak moves **right and lower**, and the curve is more spread out. The area stays the same because the number of molecules is unchanged.
- A small rise in temperature greatly increases the number of molecules with $E \ge E_a$. So a large fraction more of the collisions are **successful**, and the rate rises sharply. For many reactions, a 10 K rise roughly doubles the rate.
- Collisions also become a little more frequent, but that is a much smaller effect.
:::prac Required practical 3: effect of temperature on rate
- A typical method is sodium thiosulfate + hydrochloric acid → a sulfur precipitate. Time how long it takes for a cross under the flask to disappear, at several temperatures (use a water bath).
- Rate is proportional to 1/time. Keep the concentrations and volumes the same. Measure the temperature of the mixture itself, not the bath.
- Plot 1/t against temperature to show the trend.
:::
# Effect of concentration, pressure and surface area
- A **higher concentration** of a solution, or a **higher pressure** of a gas, puts more particles in the same volume. They collide **more frequently**, so there are more successful collisions per second. The *proportion* of molecules with $E\ge E_a$ does not change.
- A larger surface area (a powder instead of lumps) exposes more particles to collisions.
- The effect of concentration can be followed by **continuous monitoring**. An example is CaCO₃ + HCl, measuring the volume of CO₂ with a gas syringe or the loss of mass over time. Draw tangents to find the rate.
:::ex Rate from a gas syringe
Q: In the reaction of marble chips with acid, 48 cm³ of CO₂ is collected in the first 30 s. Calculate the mean rate over this time.
1. Rate = change in volume ÷ time.
2. $48 \div 30 = 1.6$.
A: 1.6 cm³ s⁻¹
:::
# Catalysts
:::def
**Catalyst:** a substance that increases the rate of a reaction without being changed in chemical composition or amount at the end.
:::
- A catalyst provides an **alternative reaction route with a lower activation energy**.
- On the Maxwell–Boltzmann curve, Eₐ moves to the **left**. The curve itself is unchanged, so many more molecules now have enough energy to react.
- A catalyst speeds up the forward and reverse reactions equally. It does not change ΔH or the position of equilibrium.
- Catalysts are important in industry: lower temperatures can be used, which saves energy and reduces CO₂ emissions.
!fig c-catalyst Energy profile with and without a catalyst
:::ex Explaining a rate change
Q: Explain why increasing the temperature from 20 °C to 30 °C roughly doubles the rate of a reaction.
1. At the higher temperature, the molecules have more kinetic energy on average.
2. On the Maxwell–Boltzmann curve, many more molecules have energy greater than or equal to Eₐ.
3. So a greater proportion of collisions are successful each second, and the frequency of collisions also increases slightly.
A: There are many more successful collisions per unit time, so the rate increases significantly.
:::
:::warn
- Saying that a higher temperature "lowers the activation energy". Only a catalyst does that.
- Drawing the hotter Maxwell–Boltzmann curve with a higher peak, or with the curve crossing back to touch the axis.
- Saying that a higher concentration gives particles more energy. It gives more collisions per second.
:::
:::tip
- Always use the phrase "more **successful** collisions **per unit time**". Frequency matters, not just "more collisions".
- When asked to sketch a curve at a higher temperature, keep the same start, make the peak lower and further right, and keep the tail above the original curve at high energy.
:::
:::sum
- A reaction needs a collision with the right orientation and $E\ge E_a$.
- The Maxwell–Boltzmann curve starts at the origin, never touches the axis, and its area is the number of molecules.
- Temperature is the biggest effect: many more molecules have at least Eₐ.
- Concentration and pressure increase collision frequency.
- Catalysts give a route with lower Eₐ and leave the curve unchanged.
:::
