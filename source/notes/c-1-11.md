@intro Electrochemical cells turn the energy of redox reactions into electricity. This topic covers electrode potentials, the standard hydrogen electrode, cell notation, calculating E°cell and predicting feasibility, and commercial cells: non-rechargeable, rechargeable and fuel cells.
# Half-cells and electrode potentials
- A **half-cell** is an element in contact with a solution of its ions, for example Zn in Zn²⁺(aq). It can also be two ions of the same element with a platinum electrode, for example Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺ with Pt.
- Two half-cells are joined by a **wire** (which carries electrons) and a **salt bridge** (filter paper soaked in KNO₃(aq), which carries ions and completes the circuit without reacting).
- **Platinum** is used when there is no solid metal in the half-cell, because it is inert and conducts.
:::def
**Standard electrode potential, E⦵:** the emf of a half-cell measured against the standard hydrogen electrode under standard conditions: 298 K, 100 kPa, and 1.00 mol dm⁻³ solutions.
:::
!fig c-she The standard hydrogen electrode (E⦵ = 0.00 V)
# Cell notation and E°cell
!fig c-cell Zinc–copper cell
- **Conventional representation.** Write the more negative electrode on the left, oxidised species next to the salt bridge: **Zn(s) | Zn²⁺(aq) || Cu²⁺(aq) | Cu(s)**. A single line is a phase boundary; a double line is the salt bridge. Pt goes on the outside, for example Pt | Fe²⁺, Fe³⁺.
$$E^\ominus_{cell}=E^\ominus_{right}-E^\ominus_{left}=E^\ominus_{(+)}-E^\ominus_{(-)}$$
- The more **negative** electrode is where **oxidation** happens; electrons flow from it through the wire to the more positive electrode.
:::ex Calculating E°cell and writing the reaction
Q: E⦵ values: Zn²⁺/Zn −0.76 V; Ag⁺/Ag +0.80 V. Find E⦵cell and write the overall equation.
1. Ag⁺/Ag is more positive, so it is the right-hand (positive) electrode, where Ag⁺ is reduced. Zn is oxidised.
2. E⦵cell = +0.80 − (−0.76) = +1.56 V.
3. Combine the half-equations with equal electrons: Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ and 2Ag⁺ + 2e⁻ → 2Ag.
A: E⦵cell = +1.56 V; Zn + 2Ag⁺ → Zn²⁺ + 2Ag
:::
# The electrochemical series and feasibility
- In a list of half-equations written as reductions and ordered by E⦵: the more **positive** E⦵, the better the **oxidising agent** (the species on the left, such as F₂ or Cl₂). The more **negative** E⦵, the better the **reducing agent** (the species on the right, such as Li or Zn).
- A reaction is **feasible** if E⦵cell is **positive**. That is the case when the oxidising agent's half-cell has a more positive E⦵ than the reducing agent's.
- Feasible reactions may still be **slow** (high activation energy). Non-standard concentrations also change the actual emf.
:::ex Predicting feasibility
Q: Will acidified MnO₄⁻ (E⦵ = +1.51 V) oxidise Cl⁻ to Cl₂ (Cl₂/Cl⁻ +1.36 V)?
1. MnO₄⁻ is reduced and Cl⁻ is oxidised.
2. E⦵cell = 1.51 − 1.36 = +0.15 V, which is positive.
A: Yes, it is feasible. (This is why KMnO₄ titrations use H₂SO₄, not HCl.)
:::
:::prac Required practical 8: measuring the emf of a cell
- Clean the metal electrodes with emery paper. Make up a salt bridge from filter paper soaked in saturated KNO₃. Connect a **high-resistance voltmeter**, so almost no current flows and you measure the emf.
- Use 1.00 mol dm⁻³ solutions at 298 K if you want standard values.
:::
# Commercial cells
- **Non-rechargeable cells** (for example alkaline zinc–manganese dioxide cells) are thrown away once the reactants are used up.
- **Rechargeable cells** have reactions that can be reversed by applying a current. In the **lithium-ion cell**, the positive electrode reaction on discharge is Li⁺ + CoO₂ + e⁻ → Li⁺[CoO₂]⁻ and the negative electrode reaction is Li → Li⁺ + e⁻. These are reversed when the cell is charged. Lithium-ion cells are used in phones and laptops. You may be given data for other cells and asked to work out their electrode reactions.
- **Fuel cells** are supplied continuously with fuel and oxygen, and are never recharged. In the **alkaline hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell**:
$$\text{negative: } \text{H}_2+2\text{OH}^-\rightarrow2\text{H}_2\text{O}+2\text{e}^-\quad(E^\ominus=-0.83\text{ V})$$
$$\text{positive: } \text{O}_2+2\text{H}_2\text{O}+4\text{e}^-\rightarrow4\text{OH}^-\quad(E^\ominus=+0.40\text{ V})$$
- The overall reaction is 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, with E⦵cell = 1.23 V.
- **Benefits and risks.** Fuel cells produce only water at the point of use and are more efficient than combustion. However, hydrogen is flammable and hard to store, and it is often made using fossil fuels. Cells in general contain toxic or flammable chemicals and need to be recycled safely.
:::warn
- Subtracting the potentials the wrong way round. Use right − left, or positive − negative. A feasible cell has a positive E⦵cell.
- Saying the salt bridge carries electrons. It carries **ions**; electrons go through the wire.
- Forgetting that changing the stoichiometric coefficients does **not** change E⦵ values.
:::
:::tip
- For "which is the strongest oxidising agent?", look for the most positive E⦵ and choose the species on the **left** of that half-equation.
- When you draw a cell, label the salt bridge, the high-resistance voltmeter, the electrodes, the solutions (1.00 mol dm⁻³) and the temperature (298 K).
:::
:::sum
- E⦵ is measured against the SHE (0.00 V) at 298 K, 100 kPa and 1.00 mol dm⁻³.
- E⦵cell = E⦵(right) − E⦵(left). The more negative electrode is oxidised, and the reaction is feasible if E⦵cell > 0.
- Lithium-ion cells are rechargeable. Fuel cells use H₂ and O₂ to make water at 1.23 V.
:::
