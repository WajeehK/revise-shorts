@intro Energetics measures the heat taken in or given out by a reaction. This topic covers enthalpy change and standard conditions, calorimetry with q = mcΔT, Hess's law cycles using formation and combustion data, and mean bond enthalpies.
# Enthalpy change
:::def
**Enthalpy change, ΔH:** the heat energy change measured at constant pressure.
**Standard conditions:** 100 kPa and a stated temperature, usually 298 K, with all substances in their standard states. Written ΔH⦵₂₉₈.
**Standard enthalpy of combustion, ΔcH⦵:** the enthalpy change when 1 mol of a substance is completely burned in oxygen, with all reactants and products in their standard states.
**Standard enthalpy of formation, ΔfH⦵:** the enthalpy change when 1 mol of a compound is formed from its elements, with all reactants and products in their standard states.
:::
- **Exothermic** reactions have negative ΔH: heat is given out and the surroundings warm up. Examples are combustion, neutralisation and respiration.
- **Endothermic** reactions have positive ΔH: heat is taken in. Examples are thermal decomposition and photosynthesis.
- ΔfH of an element in its standard state is zero by definition.

# Calorimetry
$$q=mc\Delta T\qquad \Delta H=-\frac{q}{n}$$
- Dividing the heat energy by the amount of the limiting reactant, in moles, gives the **molar enthalpy change** in kJ mol⁻¹. Remember to convert q from J to kJ, and add the sign: negative if the temperature rose (exothermic).
- $m$ is the mass of the substance being heated, usually the water or solution (1 cm³ ≈ 1 g), **not** the mass of fuel. $c$ = 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹ for water. ΔT is the temperature change in K (the same number as in °C).
- Convert $q$ from J to kJ, then divide by the moles of the limiting reactant. Add a **negative sign** if the temperature rose (exothermic).
:::ex Enthalpy of combustion from a spirit burner
Q: Burning 0.46 g of ethanol (M_r 46) heats 100 g of water from 20.0 °C to 34.5 °C. Calculate ΔcH.
1. $q=100\times4.18\times14.5=6061$ J $=6.06$ kJ.
2. $n=0.46/46=0.010$ mol.
3. $\Delta H=-6.06/0.010=-606$ kJ mol⁻¹.
A: ΔcH ≈ −610 kJ mol⁻¹ (2 s.f.). The data-book value is −1367 kJ mol⁻¹.
:::
- The experimental value is much less exothermic than the data-book value because of **heat loss** to the surroundings, **incomplete combustion**, evaporation of the fuel, and non-standard conditions.
:::prac Required practical 2: measuring an enthalpy change
- For reactions in solution, such as neutralisation or displacement with zinc and copper(II) sulfate, use a polystyrene cup with a lid as an insulated calorimeter.
- Record the temperature every 30 s for a few minutes before mixing and for several minutes after. Plot temperature against time and **extrapolate back** to the time of mixing to find the maximum temperature change, which corrects for cooling.
- Use an excess of one reagent. Stir, and use a thermometer with a fine resolution.
:::
# Hess's law
:::def
**Hess's law:** the enthalpy change for a reaction is independent of the route taken, provided the initial and final conditions are the same.
:::
!fig c-hess Hess cycles using enthalpies of formation and of combustion
:::eq
$$\Delta_rH=\sum\Delta_fH(\text{products})-\sum\Delta_fH(\text{reactants})$$
$$\Delta_rH=\sum\Delta_cH(\text{reactants})-\sum\Delta_cH(\text{products})$$
:::
:::ex Using enthalpies of formation
Q: Find ΔrH for C₂H₄(g) + H₂O(g) → C₂H₅OH(g). ΔfH values (kJ mol⁻¹): C₂H₄ +52, H₂O(g) −242, C₂H₅OH(g) −235.
1. Sum for the products: −235.
2. Sum for the reactants: +52 + (−242) = −190.
3. ΔrH = −235 − (−190) = −45.
A: −45 kJ mol⁻¹
:::
:::ex Using enthalpies of combustion
Q: Find ΔfH of methane. ΔcH values: C −394, H₂ −286, CH₄ −890 kJ mol⁻¹.
1. The formation reaction is C + 2H₂ → CH₄. The combustion route goes from these reactants via CO₂ + 2H₂O.
2. ΔfH = ΣΔcH(reactants) − ΣΔcH(products) = [−394 + 2(−286)] − (−890).
3. That is = −966 + 890 = −76.
A: −76 kJ mol⁻¹
:::
- Hess's law can also combine two measured reactions to find one that can't be measured directly. An example is the hydration of anhydrous MgSO₄ or CuSO₄: measure the enthalpy of solution of the anhydrous and the hydrated salt, then subtract.
# Mean bond enthalpies
:::def
**Mean bond enthalpy:** the enthalpy needed to break one mole of a given covalent bond in the gaseous state, averaged over a range of compounds.
:::
$$\Delta H=\sum(\text{bonds broken})-\sum(\text{bonds made})$$
- Breaking bonds is endothermic and making bonds is exothermic.
- Answers from mean bond enthalpies **differ from Hess's law values** for two reasons. The values are averages; the actual bond in a given molecule may be stronger or weaker. They also apply only to gases, so any substance that is a liquid adds an error.
:::ex Bond enthalpy calculation
Q: Estimate ΔH for CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O (all gases). Bond enthalpies (kJ mol⁻¹): C–H 413, O=O 498, C=O 805, O–H 464.
1. Bonds broken: 4(413) + 2(498) = 2648.
2. Bonds made: 2(805) + 4(464) = 3466.
3. ΔH = 2648 − 3466 = −818.
A: About −818 kJ mol⁻¹
:::
:::warn
- Using the mass of the fuel or solute as $m$ in $q=mc\Delta T$. Use the mass of the water or solution.
- Forgetting the minus sign for an exothermic reaction, or not dividing by moles.
- In a ΔfH cycle, writing reactants − products. It is **products − reactants** for formation, but **reactants − products** for combustion.
- Multiplying only some of the terms by the stoichiometric coefficients.
:::
:::tip
- Define standard enthalpies precisely: "1 mol", "standard states", "100 kPa", "298 K". Each phrase can earn a mark.
- Give ΔH with a sign, units (kJ mol⁻¹) and sensible significant figures.
:::
:::sum
- ΔH is the heat change at constant pressure: negative for exothermic, positive for endothermic.
- $q=mc\Delta T$, then $\Delta H=-q/n$. Experimental values are lower because of heat loss.
- Hess's law: ΔrH = ΣΔfH(products) − ΣΔfH(reactants), or ΣΔcH(reactants) − ΣΔcH(products).
- Bond enthalpy: broken − made. It is approximate because the values are means and apply to gases only.
:::
