@intro Some molecules exist as two forms that are mirror images of each other but cannot be superimposed, like your left and right hands. This is optical isomerism. It matters a great deal in biology and in drug design.
# Key definitions
:::def
**Stereoisomers:** compounds with the same structural formula but a different arrangement of atoms in space. Optical isomerism is one type; E/Z isomerism is the other.
**Chiral centre (asymmetric carbon):** a carbon atom bonded to **four different** atoms or groups. Mark it with an asterisk, C*.
**Enantiomers (optical isomers):** a pair of molecules that are **non-superimposable mirror images** of each other.
**Racemic mixture (racemate):** an equimolar (50 : 50) mixture of two enantiomers.
:::

# Spotting chiral centres
- Check each sp³ carbon. A carbon with two H atoms (CH₂), or with two identical groups such as two CH₃ groups, cannot be chiral.
- A carbon in a C=C or C=O is never a chiral centre, because it has only three groups and is planar.
- Look along the whole chain. In butan-2-ol, C2 carries H, OH, CH₃ and CH₂CH₃, which are all different, so C2 is chiral. Propan-2-ol has two CH₃ groups on C2, so it is not chiral.
:::ex Finding a chiral centre
Q: Is 2-hydroxypropanoic acid (lactic acid), CH₃CH(OH)COOH, chiral?
1. Look at C2. It is bonded to H, OH, CH₃ and COOH.
2. These are four different groups, so C2 is a chiral centre.
3. C1 (COOH) has only three groups, and C3 (CH₃) has three identical H atoms, so neither is chiral.
A: Yes. C2 is chiral, so lactic acid has two enantiomers.
:::
:::ex Counting chiral centres
Q: How many chiral centres are there in 2-bromo-3-chlorobutane, CH₃CHBrCHClCH₃?
1. C2 has H, Br, CH₃ and CHClCH₃: four different groups.
2. C3 has H, Cl, CH₃ and CHBrCH₃: four different groups.
A: Two chiral centres.
:::
# Drawing enantiomers
- Draw the chiral carbon as a tetrahedron: two bonds in the plane of the page, one wedge (towards you) and one dash (away from you).
- Then draw its **mirror image** across a dashed mirror line. Equivalently, swap any two groups.
- Check that the two drawings cannot be rotated to match.
# Plane-polarised light
- Each enantiomer **rotates the plane of plane-polarised light** by the same angle but in **opposite directions**. This is why enantiomers are called optically active.
- A racemic mixture is **optically inactive**, because the two rotations cancel out.
- Enantiomers have identical physical properties (melting point, boiling point, density) and identical reactions with ordinary reagents. They behave differently only towards **other chiral molecules**, such as enzymes and receptors in the body.
# Forming racemic mixtures
- The C=O group is **planar**. In nucleophilic addition, for example KCN then acid with ethanal, the CN⁻ can attack from **either face** with equal probability. This gives equal amounts of the two enantiomers of 2-hydroxypropanenitrile.
- The same happens in electrophilic addition when a planar carbocation is attacked to make a new chiral centre. For example, HBr + but-1-ene gives racemic 2-bromobutane.
- A reaction that makes only one enantiomer, such as an enzyme-catalysed reaction, gives an optically active product.
:::ex Explaining an inactive product
Q: Propanal reacts with HCN (from KCN and acid). Explain why the product is optically inactive.
1. The product, 2-hydroxybutanenitrile, has a chiral centre: C2 is bonded to H, OH, CN and C₂H₅.
2. The C=O in propanal is planar, so CN⁻ is equally likely to attack from above or below.
3. Equal amounts of both enantiomers form, giving a racemic mixture.
A: The two enantiomers rotate plane-polarised light equally in opposite directions, so the effects cancel.
:::
# Drugs and synthesis
- Often only **one enantiomer** of a drug binds to the target receptor. The other may be inactive or harmful. The thalidomide tragedy is the classic example.
- Synthetic routes through planar intermediates give racemates, which then need costly separation. Chemists use chiral catalysts or enzymes to make a single enantiomer instead.
:::warn
- Saying that enantiomers have different boiling points, or that they react differently with ordinary reagents. They do not.
- Drawing the "mirror image" as the same molecule rotated. Swap two groups or reflect it properly.
- Forgetting that a racemic mixture has **no** overall effect on plane-polarised light.
:::
:::tip
- For "explain why the product is optically inactive", make three points: the C=O (or carbocation) is planar; attack is equally likely from above and below; a racemic mixture forms, and the rotations cancel.
- Always draw enantiomers in 3D, with wedges and dashes, and a mirror line.
:::
:::sum
- A chiral carbon has four different groups. Enantiomers are non-superimposable mirror images.
- Enantiomers rotate plane-polarised light equally in opposite directions; a racemate is inactive.
- Attack on planar C=O groups or carbocations gives racemic mixtures.
- Drugs often need a single enantiomer.
:::
