# Key definitions
- **Stereoisomers:** same structural formula but a different arrangement of atoms in space. Optical isomerism is one type (E/Z is the other).
- **Chiral centre (asymmetric carbon):** a carbon atom bonded to **four different** atoms or groups. Mark it with an asterisk, C*.
- **Enantiomers (optical isomers):** a pair of molecules that are **non-superimposable mirror images** of each other.
- **Racemic mixture (racemate):** an equimolar (50 : 50) mixture of two enantiomers.

# Plane-polarised light
- Each enantiomer rotates the plane of plane-polarised light by the same angle but in **opposite directions**.
- A racemic mixture is **optically inactive**: the two rotations cancel out.
- Enantiomers have identical physical properties (melting point, boiling point, density) and identical chemical reactions, except with other chiral molecules (e.g. enzymes and receptors in the body).
# Spotting chiral centres
- Check each sp³ carbon. A carbon with two H atoms (CH₂), or two identical groups such as two CH₃ groups, cannot be chiral.
- A C=C or C=O carbon is never a chiral centre (only three groups, planar).
- Look along the whole chain: in butan-2-ol, C2 carries H, OH, CH₃ and CH₂CH₃, which are all different, so it is chiral. Propan-2-ol (two CH₃ groups) is not.
# Forming racemic mixtures
- The C=O group is **planar**. In nucleophilic addition (e.g. KCN then acid with ethanal), CN⁻ can attack from **either face** with equal probability, giving equal amounts of both enantiomers.
- The same happens in electrophilic addition when a planar carbocation is attacked to make a new chiral centre, e.g. HBr + but-1-ene → racemic 2-bromobutane.
- A reaction that makes only one enantiomer (e.g. an enzyme-catalysed reaction) gives an optically active product.
# Worked example
Is 2-hydroxypropanoic acid (lactic acid), CH₃CH(OH)COOH, chiral?
C2 is bonded to H, OH, CH₃ and COOH: four different groups, so C2 is a chiral centre and the molecule has two enantiomers. C1 (COOH) and C3 (CH₃) are not chiral.
Draw the pair as a 3D tetrahedral C with one wedge and one dash bond, then reflect it in a mirror line, so that two groups swap sides.
# Drugs and synthesis
- Often only one enantiomer of a drug binds to the target receptor. The other may be inactive or harmful (the thalidomide tragedy).
- Synthetic routes through planar intermediates give racemates, which then need costly separation; chemists use chiral catalysts or enzymes to make a single enantiomer.
# Common mistakes
- Saying enantiomers have different boiling points or react differently with ordinary reagents: they do not.
- Drawing the "mirror image" as the same molecule rotated. Swap two groups or reflect it properly.
- Forgetting that a racemic mixture has **no** effect on plane-polarised light.
# Exam tips
> For "explain why the product is optically inactive", give three points: the C=O (or carbocation) is planar; attack is equally likely from above and below; a racemic mixture forms, whose rotations cancel.
