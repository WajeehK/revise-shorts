@intro Condensation polymers such as polyesters and polyamides are made from monomers that each have two reactive groups, with a small molecule lost at every link. Their polar links also mean they can be broken down again.
# Addition vs condensation polymers
- **Addition polymers** form from alkenes; the C=C opens and nothing else is produced. The chain is non-polar C–C bonds.
- **Condensation polymers** form when two different functional groups react, losing a small molecule (usually H₂O, or HCl if an acyl chloride is used) at every link.
- Condensation monomers need **two** reactive groups each (e.g. a diol, a dicarboxylic acid, a diamine), so the chain can grow at both ends.
!fig c-polymers Ester and amide links in condensation polymers
# Polyesters
- Diol + dicarboxylic acid → polyester + water; the link is the ester group –COO–.
- **Terylene (PET):** ethane-1,2-diol HOCH₂CH₂OH + benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid HOOC–C₆H₄–COOH. Used for drinks bottles and clothing fibres.
# Polyamides
- Diamine + dicarboxylic acid (or diacyl dichloride) → polyamide; the link is the amide group –CONH–.
- **Nylon-6,6:** hexane-1,6-diamine H₂N(CH₂)₆NH₂ + hexanedioic acid HOOC(CH₂)₄COOH. Each monomer has 6 carbon atoms, hence "6,6".
- **Kevlar:** benzene-1,4-diamine + benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid. Its flat, rigid chains line up and form many hydrogen bonds between N–H and C=O on neighbouring chains, so it is very strong (bullet-proof vests).
- Proteins are natural polyamides (polypeptides) made from amino acids.
# Working out monomers from a repeat unit
- Find each ester or amide link and break it: put –OH back on the C=O side, and –H back on the O or N side.
- With a diacyl dichloride monomer, the acid end is –COCl and HCl is lost instead of H₂O.
:::ex Monomers from a repeat unit
Q: Identify the monomers of the polymer with repeat unit –NH–(CH₂)₆–NH–CO–(CH₂)₄–CO–.
1. Find the amide links, –CO–NH–, and break each one.
2. Add –H to each N and –OH to each C=O.
A: H₂N–(CH₂)₆–NH₂ (hexane-1,6-diamine) and HOOC–(CH₂)₄–COOH (hexanedioic acid), which make nylon-6,6.
:::
:::ex Drawing a polyester
Q: Draw the repeat unit of the polyester made from ethane-1,2-diol and butanedioic acid, HOOC(CH₂)₂COOH.
1. Remove the OH from the acid and the H from the alcohol at each link, losing H₂O.
2. Join them through –COO– ester links.
A: –[OCH₂CH₂O–CO(CH₂)₂CO]ₙ–
:::
:::ex Hydrolysis products
Q: Nylon-6,6 is heated with HCl(aq). What forms?
1. Acid hydrolysis breaks the amide links.
2. The diacid is released as the free acid, and the diamine forms its ammonium salt in the acid.
A: Hexanedioic acid and the hexane-1,6-diammonium chloride salt, ClH₃N(CH₂)₆NH₃Cl.
:::
# Hydrolysis and disposal
- Ester and amide links are **polar**, so they are attacked by nucleophiles: they can be hydrolysed by acid or alkali and are **biodegradable**. Poly(alkenes) have only non-polar C–C and C–H bonds, so they are not.
- Acid hydrolysis gives the dicarboxylic acid plus the diol (or the diamine as its ammonium salt); alkaline hydrolysis gives the carboxylate salt plus the diol or diamine.
- **Landfill:** cheap but uses land, and the polymer persists for years.
- **Incineration:** releases energy that can generate electricity, but produces CO₂ and possibly toxic gases (e.g. HCl from PVC), which must be removed.
- **Recycling:** saves raw materials (crude oil) and energy, but plastics must be collected and sorted, which is costly.
:::warn
- Leaving an –OH or –H on the end of a repeat unit. A repeat unit has open bonds (trailing lines) at both ends.
- Drawing the amide link backwards in the same chain; check that each link joins a C=O to an N–H.
- Calling poly(ethene) a condensation polymer. No small molecule is lost, so it is an addition polymer.
:::
:::tip
- Draw the repeat unit with the link shown in full: C=O with the O or N–H attached, then brackets and a subscript n.
- Hydrogen bonding between polyamide chains (N–H···O=C) explains the strength of Kevlar and nylon. Polyesters have only dipole–dipole forces between their C=O groups.
:::
:::sum
- Condensation polymers are made from monomers with two functional groups each, losing H₂O or HCl at every link.
- Polyesters (e.g. Terylene) have –COO– links; polyamides (nylon-6,6, Kevlar, proteins) have –CONH– links.
- Polar links can be hydrolysed, so these polymers are biodegradable. Disposal options are landfill, incineration and recycling.
:::
