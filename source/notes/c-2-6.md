# Metal-aqua ions
- [M(H₂O)₆]²⁺ is weakly acidic. [M(H₂O)₆]³⁺ is more acidic because of its higher charge density, which polarises O–H more:
- [Fe(H₂O)₆]³⁺ + H₂O ⇌ [Fe(H₂O)₅(OH)]²⁺ + H₃O⁺.
# Precipitates
- Cu²⁺: blue solution; blue Cu(H₂O)₄(OH)₂ precipitate. In excess NH₃ it forms deep blue [Cu(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺.
- Fe²⁺: green precipitate, which goes brown in air. Fe³⁺: orange-brown precipitate.
- Al³⁺: white precipitate, which dissolves in excess NaOH to give [Al(OH)₄(H₂O)₂]⁻ (amphoteric).
- With Na₂CO₃: 2+ ions give MCO₃ precipitates; 3+ ions give M(OH)₃ precipitates **plus CO₂ bubbles**.
# Ligand substitution
- [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + 4Cl⁻ ⇌ [CuCl₄]²⁻ + 6H₂O (blue → yellow-green; octahedral → tetrahedral).
# Worked example
A solution of [Fe(H₂O)₆]³⁺ has a lower pH than one of [Fe(H₂O)₆]²⁺ of the same concentration. The 3+ ion has a higher charge density, so it polarises the O–H bonds in the water ligands more, releasing H⁺ more readily.
# Common mistakes
- Writing that NH₃ dissolves all hydroxide precipitates: of the ions in this topic, excess NH₃ dissolves only the copper hydroxide precipitate (forming [Cu(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺).
- Forgetting the CO₂ bubbles when 3+ ions react with carbonate.
# Exam tips
> Learn the colours as tables (ion × reagent × few drops/excess). They are easy marks.
