@intro Metal ions in water are surrounded by water ligands, and these metal–aqua ions behave as acids. This topic covers why 3+ ions are more acidic than 2+ ions, and the test-tube reactions of Fe²⁺, Cu²⁺, Fe³⁺ and Al³⁺ with hydroxide, ammonia and carbonate.
# Metal–aqua ions and acidity
- In water, these metal ions form octahedral **[M(H₂O)₆]ⁿ⁺** ions: Fe²⁺ (pale green), Cu²⁺ (blue), Fe³⁺ (violet, often yellow-brown in solution because of hydrolysis) and Al³⁺ (colourless).
- The metal ion polarises the O–H bonds in its water ligands, so a water ligand can release H⁺:
$$[\text{Fe(H}_2\text{O)}_6]^{3+}+\text{H}_2\text{O}\rightleftharpoons[\text{Fe(H}_2\text{O)}_5\text{(OH)}]^{2+}+\text{H}_3\text{O}^+$$
- **3+ ions are more acidic than 2+ ions** (with a pH of about 3 compared with about 6). M³⁺ has a higher **charge-to-size ratio** (charge density), so it polarises the water ligands more strongly, weakening their O–H bonds and releasing H⁺ more readily.
# Reactions with hydroxide and ammonia
Both OH⁻ and NH₃ act as **bases**: they remove H⁺ ions from the water ligands until a neutral hydroxide precipitates.
| Ion | Few drops of NaOH or NH₃ | Excess NaOH | Excess NH₃ |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Fe(H₂O)₆]²⁺ (green) | green precipitate of Fe(H₂O)₄(OH)₂; it turns brown in air (oxidised to Fe(III)) | no change | no change |
| [Cu(H₂O)₆]²⁺ (blue) | blue precipitate of Cu(H₂O)₄(OH)₂ | no change | dissolves to give a **deep blue** solution of [Cu(NH₃)₄(H₂O)₂]²⁺ |
| [Fe(H₂O)₆]³⁺ (yellow/violet) | brown precipitate of Fe(H₂O)₃(OH)₃ | no change | no change |
| [Al(H₂O)₆]³⁺ (colourless) | white precipitate of Al(H₂O)₃(OH)₃ | dissolves to give a colourless solution of [Al(OH)₄(H₂O)₂]⁻ | no change |
:::eq
$$[\text{Cu(H}_2\text{O)}_6]^{2+}+2\text{OH}^-\rightarrow\text{Cu(H}_2\text{O)}_4\text{(OH)}_2+2\text{H}_2\text{O}$$
$$[\text{Fe(H}_2\text{O)}_6]^{3+}+3\text{NH}_3\rightarrow\text{Fe(H}_2\text{O)}_3\text{(OH)}_3+3\text{NH}_4^+$$
$$\text{Al(H}_2\text{O)}_3\text{(OH)}_3+\text{OH}^-\rightarrow[\text{Al(OH)}_4\text{(H}_2\text{O)}_2]^-+\text{H}_2\text{O}$$
$$\text{Cu(H}_2\text{O)}_4\text{(OH)}_2+4\text{NH}_3\rightarrow[\text{Cu(NH}_3)_4\text{(H}_2\text{O)}_2]^{2+}+2\text{H}_2\text{O}+2\text{OH}^-$$
:::
- **Amphoteric** behaviour: aluminium hydroxide dissolves in both acid and excess alkali. With acid: Al(H₂O)₃(OH)₃ + 3H⁺ → [Al(H₂O)₆]³⁺.
- Note that excess ammonia does **ligand substitution** only with copper here. Excess NaOH removes a further proton only from aluminium.
# Reactions with carbonate
- **2+ ions** (weakly acidic) form **metal carbonate** precipitates: [Fe(H₂O)₆]²⁺ + CO₃²⁻ → FeCO₃ + 6H₂O, a green precipitate. Cu²⁺ gives a blue-green precipitate of CuCO₃.
- **3+ ions** are acidic enough to react with carbonate to release **CO₂ gas** (effervescence), forming the hydroxide precipitate instead:
$$2[\text{Fe(H}_2\text{O)}_6]^{3+}+3\text{CO}_3^{2-}\rightarrow2\text{Fe(H}_2\text{O)}_3\text{(OH)}_3+3\text{CO}_2+3\text{H}_2\text{O}$$
- So Fe³⁺ gives a brown precipitate with bubbles, and Al³⁺ gives a white precipitate with bubbles.
:::ex Identifying an ion
Q: A solution gives a white precipitate with a few drops of NaOH(aq). The precipitate dissolves in excess NaOH but not in excess ammonia. With Na₂CO₃ it gives a white precipitate and bubbles. Identify the ion and explain the bubbles.
1. A white precipitate that dissolves in excess NaOH is amphoteric Al(OH)₃.
2. The bubbles are CO₂. Al³⁺ is a 3+ ion, acidic enough to react with carbonate.
A: [Al(H₂O)₆]³⁺. The acidic 3+ aqua ion releases H₃O⁺, which reacts with CO₃²⁻ to give CO₂.
:::
:::ex Explaining relative acidity
Q: Explain why a solution of iron(III) chloride is more acidic than a solution of iron(II) chloride of the same concentration.
1. Fe³⁺ has a higher charge and a smaller radius than Fe²⁺, so its charge density is higher.
2. It polarises the O–H bonds of the water ligands more, so H⁺ is released more readily.
A: [Fe(H₂O)₆]³⁺ dissociates to a greater extent, so [H₃O⁺] is higher and the pH is lower.
:::
:::prac Required practical 11: identifying transition metal ions
- Add NaOH(aq) dropwise, then in excess. Repeat with NH₃(aq) dropwise, then in excess. Repeat with Na₂CO₃(aq). Record the colours of the precipitates and solutions and any effervescence.
- Leave the iron(II) hydroxide precipitate standing to see it turn brown as it is oxidised in air.
:::
:::warn
- Writing "Fe(OH)₂" without water ligands is acceptable in some answers, but AQA usually expects the full formulae, Fe(H₂O)₄(OH)₂ and so on.
- Saying that Fe²⁺ produces CO₂ with carbonate. Only 3+ ions do.
- Forgetting the charges when you balance the equation for the Cu–NH₃ substitution.
:::
:::tip
- Learn the table as a grid of colours: Fe²⁺ green, Cu²⁺ blue, Fe³⁺ brown, Al³⁺ white. The excess reactions are only Cu with NH₃ (deep blue) and Al with NaOH (colourless).
:::
:::sum
- [M(H₂O)₆]³⁺ is more acidic than [M(H₂O)₆]²⁺ because of its higher charge density.
- OH⁻ and NH₃ deprotonate the aqua ions to give hydroxide precipitates. Excess NH₃ gives deep blue with Cu; excess OH⁻ dissolves Al(OH)₃.
- Carbonate gives MCO₃ with 2+ ions, but M(OH)₃ and CO₂ with 3+ ions.
:::
