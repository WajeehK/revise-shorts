@intro Amines are organic derivatives of ammonia in which one or more H atoms are replaced by carbon groups. The lone pair on nitrogen makes them both bases and nucleophiles.
# Classifying and naming
| Type | Structure | Example |
|---|---|---|
| primary | RNH₂ | ethylamine, CH₃CH₂NH₂ |
| secondary | R₂NH | diethylamine |
| tertiary | R₃N | trimethylamine |
| quaternary ammonium ion | R₄N⁺ | tetramethylammonium |
- **Phenylamine**, C₆H₅NH₂, is the simplest aromatic amine.
# Preparation
**Aliphatic amines**
- From a **halogenoalkane with excess ammonia** (in ethanol, heated in a sealed tube): CH₃CH₂Br + 2NH₃ → CH₃CH₂NH₂ + NH₄Br. Excess NH₃ reduces further substitution, but the product is usually a mixture.
- By **reducing nitriles** with LiAlH₄ in dry ether, or with H₂ and a Ni catalyst: CH₃CN + 4[H] → CH₃CH₂NH₂. This gives only the primary amine, which is better for synthesis.
**Aromatic amines**
- Reduce **nitrobenzene** with **tin and concentrated HCl**, then add NaOH: C₆H₅NO₂ + 6[H] → C₆H₅NH₂ + 2H₂O.
- Aromatic amines are used to make **dyes**.
# Amines as bases
- The lone pair on N accepts a proton: RNH₂ + H₂O ⇌ RNH₃⁺ + OH⁻. With acids, amines form salts: CH₃NH₂ + HCl → CH₃NH₃⁺Cl⁻.
- Base strength depends on how **available the lone pair** is:
| Order | Reason |
|---|---|
| primary aliphatic amines (strongest) | the alkyl group is **electron-donating** (positive inductive effect), so it increases electron density on N |
| ammonia | — |
| aromatic amines (weakest) | the N lone pair is **delocalised into the benzene ring**, so it is less available |
:::ex Ranking base strength
Q: Put ammonia, phenylamine and propylamine in order of base strength, strongest first, and explain.
1. Propylamine: the propyl group pushes electrons onto N, so the lone pair is more available to accept H⁺.
2. Ammonia has no alkyl group.
3. Phenylamine: the lone pair overlaps with the ring's π system, so it is delocalised and less available.
A: propylamine > ammonia > phenylamine
:::
# Amines as nucleophiles
**Nucleophilic substitution with halogenoalkanes**
- A primary amine reacts again with the halogenoalkane, giving a secondary amine, then a tertiary amine, then a **quaternary ammonium salt**. For example, (CH₃)₃N + CH₃Br → (CH₃)₄N⁺Br⁻.
- Mechanism: the N lone pair attacks the δ+ carbon and the halide leaves. Then a second amine or NH₃ molecule removes H⁺ from the N.
- Quaternary ammonium salts with long alkyl chains are **cationic surfactants**, used in fabric softeners and hair conditioners.
**Nucleophilic addition–elimination with acyl chlorides and anhydrides**
- These reactions give **N-substituted amides**: CH₃COCl + 2CH₃CH₂NH₂ → CH₃CONHCH₂CH₃ + CH₃CH₂NH₃⁺Cl⁻.
- The mechanism is the same as for acyl chlorides with NH₃ (see the Carboxylic acids topic).
:::ex Making a quaternary salt
Q: Excess bromoethane reacts with ammonia. Name the final product.
1. Each substitution replaces one H on the N with an ethyl group: NH₃ → EtNH₂ → Et₂NH → Et₃N.
2. Et₃N attacks a fourth bromoethane, giving Et₄N⁺, which has no H left to lose.
A: Tetraethylammonium bromide, (CH₃CH₂)₄N⁺Br⁻.
:::
:::ex Choosing a synthesis route
Q: Give two routes from 1-bromopropane to an amine, and say which gives a purer primary amine.
1. Route A: excess NH₃ in ethanol, heated, gives propylamine, contaminated with secondary and tertiary amines.
2. Route B: KCN in ethanol under reflux gives butanenitrile; reduction with LiAlH₄ or H₂/Ni then gives butylamine. This adds one carbon.
A: Route B gives only a primary amine (with one more carbon). Route A gives a mixture.
:::
:::warn
- Saying that phenylamine is a stronger base "because benzene is electron-withdrawing" without mentioning **delocalisation of the lone pair**.
- Forgetting that ammonia or amine must be in excess, and that a second molecule removes the H⁺.
- Mixing up the reagents: Sn/HCl reduces nitrobenzene; LiAlH₄ or H₂/Ni reduces nitriles.
:::
:::tip
- In base-strength answers, always describe **lone-pair availability** on N.
- Remember that the nitrile route extends the carbon chain by one.
:::
:::sum
- Amines are primary, secondary or tertiary, plus quaternary ammonium ions.
- Preparation: halogenoalkane + excess NH₃; nitrile + LiAlH₄ or H₂/Ni; nitrobenzene + Sn/HCl.
- Base strength: aliphatic amines > NH₃ > aromatic amines, depending on lone-pair availability.
- As nucleophiles, amines give secondary and tertiary amines, quaternary salts (surfactants) and N-substituted amides.
:::
