@intro Gravity holds planets, moons and satellites in orbit. This topic covers gravitational fields, Newton's inverse-square law, Kepler's laws, satellites, and gravitational potential and escape velocity.
# Gravitational fields
:::def
**Gravitational field strength (g):** the force per unit mass at a point, $g=F/m$ (in N kg⁻¹).
**Gravitational field:** a region in which a mass experiences a force.
:::
- Field lines show the direction of the force on a mass. A spherical mass behaves as if all its mass were at its centre, giving a **radial** field.
- Close to the Earth's surface, the field is approximately **uniform** (parallel lines), with g = 9.81 N kg⁻¹.
!fig p-gfield Radial field of a planet and the uniform field near its surface
# Newton's law of gravitation
:::eq
$$F=-\frac{GMm}{r^2}\qquad g=-\frac{GM}{r^2}\qquad G=6.67\times10^{-11}\text{ N m}^2\text{ kg}^{-2}$$
:::
- The force is **attractive** and follows an **inverse-square law**: doubling r makes F one quarter as large. The minus sign shows attraction.
:::ex Field strength of the Moon
Q: Find g at the Moon's surface (M = 7.35 × 10²² kg, r = 1.74 × 10⁶ m).
1. g = GM/r² = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 7.35 × 10²² / (1.74 × 10⁶)².
A: g = 1.62 N kg⁻¹, about one sixth of the Earth's.
:::
# Planetary motion and satellites
- **Kepler's laws:**
1. Planets move in ellipses, with the Sun at one focus.
2. A line from the Sun to a planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times.
3. **T² ∝ r³**.
- For a circular orbit, gravity provides the centripetal force: GMm/r² = mv²/r, which gives $T^2=\frac{4\pi^2}{GM}r^3$.
- **Geostationary satellites** orbit above the equator with T = 24 h, moving in the same direction as the Earth spins, at about 36 000 km above the surface. They stay above the same point, so they are useful for communications.
:::ex Geostationary orbit
Q: Find the orbital radius of a geostationary satellite (M_E = 5.97 × 10²⁴ kg).
1. r³ = GMT²/(4π²) = 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 5.97 × 10²⁴ × (86 400)² / 39.5.
2. r³ = 7.5 × 10²², so r = 4.2 × 10⁷ m.
A: About 4.2 × 10⁷ m from the Earth's centre.
:::
:::ex Orbital speed
Q: Find the speed of a satellite in a circular orbit 400 km above the Earth's surface.
1. r = 6.37 × 10⁶ + 0.40 × 10⁶ = 6.77 × 10⁶ m.
2. v = √(GM/r) = √(6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 5.97 × 10²⁴ / 6.77 × 10⁶).
A: v = 7.7 × 10³ m s⁻¹. Lower orbits are faster.
:::
# Gravitational potential and energy
:::def
**Gravitational potential (V_g):** the work done per unit mass to bring a small mass from infinity to that point.
:::
:::eq
$$V_g=-\frac{GM}{r}\qquad E=mV_g=-\frac{GMm}{r}\qquad v_\text{esc}=\sqrt{\frac{2GM}{r}}$$
:::
- The potential is **zero at infinity** and negative everywhere else.
- Work done moving a mass m = m ΔV_g. On a force–distance graph, the area under the curve is the work done.
- **Escape velocity** is the minimum speed needed to escape from the surface to infinity: ½mv² = GMm/r.
:::ex Escape velocity from the Earth
Q: Find the Earth's escape velocity (r = 6.37 × 10⁶ m).
1. v = √(2 × 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ × 5.97 × 10²⁴ / 6.37 × 10⁶).
A: v = 1.12 × 10⁴ m s⁻¹ (11.2 km s⁻¹)
:::
:::warn
- Using the height above the surface instead of the distance from the centre for r.
- Writing the potential as positive. It is always negative, rising to zero at infinity.
- Forgetting to cube or square r in Kepler's third law.
:::
:::tip
- For any orbit question, start from GMm/r² = mv²/r = mω²r.
:::
:::sum
- g = F/m = GM/r²; F = GMm/r² (inverse square).
- Kepler: T² ∝ r³. Geostationary orbits have T = 24 h, above the equator.
- V_g = −GM/r; v_esc = √(2GM/r).
:::
