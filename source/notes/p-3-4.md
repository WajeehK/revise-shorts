@spec OCR H556 3.4.1 Springs; 3.4.2 Mechanical properties of matter (PAG 2)
@intro Materials stretch when a force acts on them. This topic covers Hooke's law, elastic potential energy, and stress, strain and the Young modulus, which describe a material regardless of its size.
# Springs and Hooke's law
:::def
**Hooke's law:** the extension of a spring is directly proportional to the force applied, provided the limit of proportionality is not exceeded: $F=kx$.
**Force constant (k):** the force per unit extension (in N m⁻¹). A stiffer spring has a larger k.
**Elastic deformation:** the object returns to its original shape when the force is removed.
**Plastic deformation:** permanent deformation, which remains after the force is removed.
:::
!fig p-force-ext Force–extension graph, showing the limit of proportionality
- Springs **in series**: $1/k=1/k_1+1/k_2$, so the combination is less stiff. Springs **in parallel**: $k=k_1+k_2$.
# Elastic potential energy
- The work done in stretching = the **area under the force–extension graph**. In the Hooke's law region:
$$E=\tfrac12Fx=\tfrac12kx^2$$
- During loading and unloading, if the unloading curve is different (as for rubber), the area between the curves is the energy transferred to thermal energy.
:::ex Spring energy
Q: A spring with k = 200 N m⁻¹ is stretched by 0.15 m. Find the energy stored.
1. E = ½kx² = ½ × 200 × 0.15².
2. E = 100 × 0.0225 = 2.25 J.
A: 2.25 J
:::
:::ex Springs in series and parallel
Q: Two springs, each with k = 100 N m⁻¹, are joined (a) in series and (b) in parallel. Find the combined force constant in each case.
1. Series: 1/k = 1/100 + 1/100, so k = 50 N m⁻¹.
2. Parallel: k = 100 + 100 = 200 N m⁻¹.
A: (a) 50 N m⁻¹ (b) 200 N m⁻¹
:::
# Stress, strain and the Young modulus
:::eq
$$\text{stress }\sigma=\frac{F}{A}\ (\text{Pa})\qquad\text{strain }\varepsilon=\frac{x}{L}\ (\text{no units})\qquad E=\frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon}$$
:::
- **Ultimate tensile strength (UTS):** the maximum stress a material can withstand before breaking.
!fig p-stress-strain Stress–strain graphs for different types of material
| Material type | Behaviour | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **ductile** | large plastic region; can be drawn into wires | copper |
| **brittle** | breaks with little or no plastic deformation | glass, cast iron |
| **polymeric** | large extension; rubber shows hysteresis | rubber, polythene |
:::ex Young modulus
Q: A 2.0 m steel wire of cross-sectional area 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ m² stretches 2.0 mm under a 20 N load. Find E.
1. σ = 20 / 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ = 2.0 × 10⁸ Pa.
2. ε = 2.0 × 10⁻³ / 2.0 = 1.0 × 10⁻³.
3. E = σ/ε = 2.0 × 10⁸ / 1.0 × 10⁻³ = 2.0 × 10¹¹ Pa.
A: E = 2.0 × 10¹¹ Pa, a typical value for steel.
:::
:::prac PAG 2: determining the Young modulus
- Use a long, thin wire, clamped at one end and passing over a pulley, with a marker against a fixed scale. Alternatively, use Searle's apparatus.
- Measure the diameter with a **micrometer** at several points along the wire and take the mean. Then A = πd²/4.
- Add masses in steps. Record the extension each time, then unload to check for plastic deformation.
- Plot stress against strain. The gradient of the linear region is E.
- Wear **goggles**, because the wire may snap, and keep feet clear of the masses.
:::
- **Compression** is the opposite of extension: a compressive force shortens a spring or sample, and Hooke's law applies to compression in the same way, with x the reduction in length.
:::warn
- Using the diameter instead of the radius in A = πr² (or forgetting to square it).
- Forgetting to convert mm to m before calculating strain.
- Saying that "strain has units of m". It is a ratio, so it has no units.
:::
:::tip
- The gradient of a force–extension graph is k, but the gradient of a stress–strain graph is E. Don't confuse them.
:::
:::sum
- F = kx up to the limit of proportionality; E = ½kx² is the area under the F–x graph.
- Stress = F/A, strain = x/L, and E = stress/strain.
- Ductile, brittle and polymeric materials have characteristic stress–strain curves.
:::
