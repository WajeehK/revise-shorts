@intro Chromatography separates the substances in a mixture so that they can be identified and their purity checked. It is often the first step before spectroscopy.
# The principle
- Every chromatography method has a **stationary phase** and a **mobile phase**.
- Components separate because they have different balances between **solubility in the mobile phase** and **retention by (adsorption onto) the stationary phase**. A component that is held more strongly moves more slowly.
# Thin-layer chromatography (TLC)
- The stationary phase is a thin layer of **silica (SiO₂) or alumina (Al₂O₃)** on a glass or plastic plate. The mobile phase is a solvent.
!fig c-tlc A developed TLC plate showing R_f measurement
:::eq
$$R_f=\frac{\text{distance moved by spot}}{\text{distance moved by solvent front}}$$
:::
- Colourless spots are located with **UV light**, if the plate has a fluorescent layer, or by using a chemical locating agent such as iodine or ninhydrin.
- To identify a compound, compare its R_f with that of a known substance **in the same solvent and on the same type of plate**.
:::ex R_f calculation
Q: A spot moves 3.6 cm while the solvent front moves 8.0 cm. Calculate R_f.
1. R_f = 3.6 / 8.0 = 0.45.
A: R_f = 0.45 (no units, always between 0 and 1).
:::
# Column chromatography (CC)
- A column is packed with **silica or alumina** as the stationary phase, and a solvent (the eluent) is run through it.
- The components come out at different times and are collected in separate fractions. This is used to **purify** products on a larger scale.
# Gas chromatography (GC)
- The stationary phase is a liquid or solid coated inside a long, thin column in an oven. The mobile phase is an **unreactive carrier gas**, such as He or N₂.
- The **retention time** is the time from injection to detection. It identifies a component when compared with known standards under the same conditions.
- The **area under each peak** is proportional to the amount of that component.
- **GC–MS:** the GC separates the components, then the mass spectrometer identifies each one from its molecular ion and fragmentation, by comparison with a database. It is used in forensics, airport security and drug testing.
:::ex Interpreting a gas chromatogram
Q: A gas chromatogram has two peaks with areas of 30 and 90 units. What percentage of the mixture is the second component?
1. The total area is 120.
2. The second component is 90 / 120 = 75%.
A: 75% (assuming the detector responds equally to both components).
:::
:::prac Required practical 12: separation of species by TLC
- Draw the TLC baseline in **pencil**, because ink would dissolve and run. Keep it above the solvent level.
- Cover the tank so that the atmosphere is saturated with solvent vapour.
- Mark the solvent front as soon as you remove the plate.
- One spot in several different solvents suggests a pure compound.
- Wear gloves, because locating agents and solvents are harmful, and handle the plate by its edges so that you do not contaminate it.
- Two-way TLC: run the plate in one solvent, turn it through 90° and run it again in a second solvent. This separates spots that overlap in the first solvent.
:::
:::warn
- Drawing the baseline in pen, or putting it below the solvent level.
- Calculating R_f from the top of the spot. Measure to the **centre** of the spot.
- Saying that a longer retention time means "more of the substance". The amount comes from the **peak area**.
:::
:::tip
- Explain separation in terms of the relative affinity of each component for the stationary phase and the mobile phase.
:::
:::sum
- Every method has a stationary phase and a mobile phase; components separate by their relative affinity for each.
- TLC uses R_f = spot distance ÷ solvent distance.
- Column chromatography purifies larger amounts.
- GC uses retention time and peak area; GC–MS identifies the components.
:::
