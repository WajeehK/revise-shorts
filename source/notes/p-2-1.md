@spec OCR H556 2.1.1 Physical quantities; 2.1.2 S.I. units
@intro Every measurement in physics is a number with a unit. This topic covers the base S.I. units, how other units are built from them, prefixes, and checking equations for homogeneity.
# Physical quantities
- A **physical quantity** has a **numerical value and a unit**, for example 9.81 m s⁻².
- You should be able to **estimate** physical quantities to an order of magnitude. A typical person is about 70 kg, a car travels at about 30 m s⁻¹ on a motorway, a mains supply is 230 V, and the wavelength of visible light is about 500 nm.
:::ex Estimating a quantity
Q: Estimate the kinetic energy of a sprinter in a 100 m race.
1. Assume a mass of about 70 kg and a top speed of about 10 m s⁻¹.
2. E = ½mv² = ½ × 70 × 10² = 3500 J.
A: About 4 × 10³ J (an order of magnitude of 10³ J).
:::
# S.I. base units
| Quantity | Base unit | Symbol |
|---|---|---|
| mass | kilogram | kg |
| length | metre | m |
| time | second | s |
| current | ampere | A |
| temperature | kelvin | K |
| amount of substance | mole | mol |
- All other units are **derived units**, built from base units. For example, 1 N = 1 kg m s⁻², 1 J = 1 kg m² s⁻², 1 W = 1 kg m² s⁻³, 1 C = 1 A s, and 1 V = 1 J C⁻¹ = 1 kg m² s⁻³ A⁻¹.
:::ex Finding base units
Q: Express the ohm (Ω) in S.I. base units.
1. $R=V/I$, and V has base units kg m² s⁻³ A⁻¹.
2. Divide by A.
A: 1 Ω = 1 kg m² s⁻³ A⁻².
:::
# Homogeneity of equations
- In a physically correct equation, **every term has the same base units**. This is called homogeneity. Checking it can show that an equation is wrong, but it cannot prove an equation right, because dimensionless constants (such as ½) cannot be checked.
:::ex Checking an equation
Q: Show that $s=ut+\tfrac12at^2$ is homogeneous.
1. s has units of m.
2. ut has units of m s⁻¹ × s = m.
3. at² has units of m s⁻² × s² = m.
A: Every term has units of m, so the equation is homogeneous.
:::
# Prefixes
| Prefix | Symbol | Factor |
|---|---|---|
| pico | p | 10⁻¹² |
| nano | n | 10⁻⁹ |
| micro | μ | 10⁻⁶ |
| milli | m | 10⁻³ |
| centi | c | 10⁻² |
| kilo | k | 10³ |
| mega | M | 10⁶ |
| giga | G | 10⁹ |
| tera | T | 10¹² |
:::ex Converting units
Q: Convert a density of 2.7 g cm⁻³ to kg m⁻³.
1. 1 g = 10⁻³ kg, and 1 cm³ = (10⁻² m)³ = 10⁻⁶ m³.
2. 2.7 × 10⁻³ / 10⁻⁶ = 2.7 × 10³.
A: 2700 kg m⁻³.
:::
- **Graph and table conventions.** Label graph axes and table columns as quantity / unit, for example "time / s" or "current / mA". Dividing by the unit means the numbers on the axis or in the table column are pure numbers.
:::warn
- Converting area or volume units with the linear factor. For example, 1 cm² = 10⁻⁴ m², **not** 10⁻² m².
- Treating the joule or the newton as base units. They are derived.
- Confusing m (milli) with M (mega).
:::
:::tip
- Write the quantity in standard form with the unit **before** calculating, to avoid prefix slips.
- In estimates, show one significant figure and state your assumptions.
:::
:::sum
- The six base units are kg, m, s, A, K and mol; all others are derived.
- A homogeneous equation has the same base units in every term.
- Learn the prefixes from pico to tera, and square or cube them for area and volume.
:::
