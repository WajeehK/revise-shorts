@spec AQA 7405 3.1.2.1–3.1.2.5 (Required practical 1)
@intro The mole lets chemists count particles by weighing them. This topic covers relative masses, the Avogadro constant, concentrations, the ideal gas equation, empirical and molecular formulae, and calculations from balanced equations, including yield, atom economy and titrations.
# Relative masses
:::def
**Relative atomic mass, $A_r$:** the average mass of an atom of an element, compared with 1/12 of the mass of an atom of carbon-12.
**Relative molecular mass, $M_r$:** the average mass of a molecule, compared with 1/12 of the mass of an atom of carbon-12.
**Relative formula mass:** the same idea, used for ionic compounds, which do not exist as molecules.
:::
- $M_r$ is the sum of the $A_r$ values in the formula. For example, $M_r(\text{CaCO}_3)=40.1+12.0+3(16.0)=100.1$.
- Relative masses have **no units**. Molar mass $M$ has the same value but with units of g mol⁻¹.
# The mole and the Avogadro constant
:::def
**Avogadro constant, $L$ (or $N_A$):** the number of particles in one mole, $6.022\times10^{23}$ mol⁻¹. You don't need to memorise it; it is given in the data booklet.
**Mole:** the amount of substance containing $6.022\times10^{23}$ particles (atoms, molecules, ions or electrons, which you must name).
:::
:::eq
$$n=\frac{m}{M}\qquad N=n\times L\qquad n=c\times V\ (V\text{ in dm}^3)$$
:::
- Volumes in cm³ must be divided by 1000 to give dm³. Concentration is in mol dm⁻³.
- Always state which particle you are counting. One mole of H₂O contains 3 moles of atoms.
# The ideal gas equation
$$pV=nRT$$
- SI units are essential: $p$ in **Pa**, $V$ in **m³**, $T$ in **K**, and $R = 8.31$ J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ (given).
- Conversions: 1 kPa = 10³ Pa; 1 dm³ = 10⁻³ m³; 1 cm³ = 10⁻⁶ m³; T(K) = θ(°C) + 273.
- A typical use is finding the $M_r$ of a volatile liquid. You measure the mass vaporised and the volume of gas, find $n=pV/RT$, then $M_r=m/n$.
:::ex Ideal gas calculation
Q: 0.250 g of a volatile liquid vaporises to give 98.0 cm³ of gas at 101 kPa and 373 K. Find its $M_r$.
1. Convert to SI units: $V=98.0\times10^{-6}$ m³ and $p=101\times10^3$ Pa.
2. $n=\frac{pV}{RT}=\frac{101000\times98.0\times10^{-6}}{8.31\times373}=3.19\times10^{-3}$ mol.
3. $M_r=\frac{m}{n}=\frac{0.250}{3.19\times10^{-3}}=78.3$.
A: $M_r \approx 78$
:::
# Empirical and molecular formulae
:::def
**Empirical formula:** the simplest whole-number ratio of atoms of each element in a compound.
**Molecular formula:** the actual number of atoms of each element in one molecule.
:::
1. Divide the mass (or percentage) of each element by its $A_r$.
2. Divide each answer by the smallest one.
3. If necessary, multiply to get whole numbers (a ratio of 1.5 means ×2, 1.33 means ×3).
4. Molecular formula = empirical formula × $\frac{M_r}{M_r(\text{empirical})}$.
:::ex Empirical and molecular formula
Q: A compound contains 85.7% C and 14.3% H by mass, and its $M_r$ is 56.0. Find its molecular formula.
1. Moles: C $=85.7/12.0=7.14$; H $=14.3/1.0=14.3$.
2. Ratio: divide by 7.14 to get C : H = 1 : 2. The empirical formula is CH₂ (mass 14.0).
3. $56.0/14.0=4$, so multiply by 4.
A: C₄H₈
:::
# Balanced equations and calculations
- Balance atoms first, then check that charges balance in **ionic equations**. Spectator ions are left out. For example, the ionic equation for neutralisation is H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H₂O(l).
- To go from one substance to another: moles of the known substance → mole ratio from the equation → moles of the unknown → mass, volume or concentration.
:::eq
$$\text{percentage yield}=\frac{\text{actual mass of product}}{\text{theoretical mass of product}}\times100$$
$$\text{percentage atom economy}=\frac{M_r\text{ of desired product}}{\sum M_r\text{ of all reactants}}\times100$$
:::
- **High atom economy** means less waste and fewer by-products to separate or dispose of. That saves money and raw materials and is better for the environment. Addition reactions have 100% atom economy.
- Yield and atom economy are different. A reaction can have a high yield but a poor atom economy.
- **Water of crystallisation.** Heat a hydrated salt to constant mass. Find the moles of anhydrous salt and the moles of water lost, then the ratio gives $x$ in, for example, MgSO₄·$x$H₂O.
:::ex Titration calculation
Q: 25.0 cm³ of sodium carbonate solution reacts with 22.50 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ HCl. The equation is Na₂CO₃ + 2HCl → 2NaCl + H₂O + CO₂. Find the concentration of the Na₂CO₃.
1. Moles of HCl $=0.100\times\frac{22.50}{1000}=2.25\times10^{-3}$ mol.
2. The ratio is 1 Na₂CO₃ : 2 HCl, so moles of Na₂CO₃ $=1.125\times10^{-3}$ mol.
3. $c=\frac{1.125\times10^{-3}}{0.0250}=0.0450$ mol dm⁻³.
A: 0.0450 mol dm⁻³
:::
:::prac Required practical 1: making a standard solution and titrating
- Weigh the solid by difference. Dissolve it in a beaker of distilled water, transfer it to a **volumetric flask** with washings, make it up to the mark (bottom of the meniscus at eye level), then invert to mix.
- Rinse the pipette and burette with the solutions they will hold. Use an indicator and add dropwise near the end point.
- Repeat until you have **concordant titres** (within 0.10 cm³). Ignore the rough titre and any outliers when you calculate the mean titre.
- The uncertainty in a titre is ±0.05 cm³ per reading × 2 readings = ±0.10 cm³. Percentage uncertainty = uncertainty ÷ titre × 100.
:::
:::warn
- Forgetting to convert cm³ to dm³ (÷1000), or °C to K, or kPa to Pa in $pV=nRT$.
- Using the mole ratio the wrong way round. Write the ratio under the equation before you start.
- Rounding too early. Keep extra figures until the final answer, then give the answer to the same number of significant figures as the least precise data.
:::
:::tip
- Lay out multi-step calculations as "moles → ratio → moles → answer", with units at every step. Method marks are awarded even if the final answer is wrong.
- In atom economy, use the **balanced** equation and include every reactant.
:::
:::sum
- $n=m/M$, $N=nL$, $n=cV$ and $pV=nRT$ (in SI units).
- Empirical formula: divide by $A_r$, then divide by the smallest. Molecular formula = empirical × ($M_r$ ÷ empirical mass).
- Use the mole ratio from the balanced equation to find masses, gas volumes and concentrations.
- Yield compares actual with theoretical product; atom economy compares desired product with all reactants.
:::
