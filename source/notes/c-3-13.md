@intro This topic applies organic chemistry to biology: amino acids and proteins, how enzymes work, the structure of DNA, and how the anticancer drug cisplatin acts on DNA.
# Amino acids
- General structure: **H₂N–CHR–COOH**. Each amino acid has an amine group and a carboxylic acid group on the same carbon. Most are chiral, because the central carbon has H, NH₂, COOH and R.
- In the solid and in neutral solution they exist as **zwitterions**, ⁺H₃N–CHR–COO⁻. The ionic attractions between zwitterions give amino acids high melting points.
| Conditions | Form |
|---|---|
| acidic (low pH) | ⁺H₃N–CHR–COOH: the COO⁻ is protonated |
| at the isoelectric point | ⁺H₃N–CHR–COO⁻ (zwitterion) |
| alkaline (high pH) | H₂N–CHR–COO⁻: the NH₃⁺ loses H⁺ |
:::ex Forms of an amino acid at different pH
Q: Draw alanine, H₂NCH(CH₃)COOH, as it exists at pH 1 and at pH 13.
1. At pH 1 (excess H⁺), both groups are protonated: ⁺H₃NCH(CH₃)COOH.
2. At pH 13 (excess OH⁻), both groups are deprotonated: H₂NCH(CH₃)COO⁻.
A: pH 1: ⁺H₃NCH(CH₃)COOH. pH 13: H₂NCH(CH₃)COO⁻.
:::
# Proteins
- Proteins are condensation polymers of amino acids joined by **peptide (amide) links**, –CONH–, with H₂O lost at each link.
- **Hydrolysis**, by heating with 6 mol dm⁻³ HCl for several hours, breaks the peptide links and releases the amino acids, which are present as their ammonium salts in the acid.
| Level | What it is | Held by |
|---|---|---|
| primary | the sequence of amino acids | covalent peptide bonds |
| secondary | **α-helix** or **β-pleated sheet** | **hydrogen bonds** between C=O and N–H of the backbone |
| tertiary | the overall 3D folding of the chain | H-bonds, ionic attractions, and **disulfide bridges** (–S–S– between cysteine residues) |
- **Thin-layer chromatography** separates and identifies the amino acids from a hydrolysed protein. Amino acids are colourless, so they are located with **ninhydrin** spray (which gives purple spots) or with UV light. Each is identified by its R_f value.
:::ex Dipeptides
Q: How many different dipeptides can form from one glycine and one alanine?
1. Either the NH₂ of glycine joins the COOH of alanine, or the other way round.
2. That gives Gly-Ala and Ala-Gly, which have different sequences.
A: Two dipeptides.
:::
# Enzymes
- Enzymes are protein catalysts. Each has an **active site** with a specific 3D shape that is **stereospecific**: it binds only one enantiomer of a substrate.
- **Inhibitors** are drugs that block the active site. They have a similar shape to the substrate and bind to the active site, stopping the substrate from binding.
- Computer modelling helps design drugs that fit a target active site.
# DNA
- DNA is a polymer of **nucleotides**. Each nucleotide is a **phosphate** group, the sugar **2-deoxyribose** and one of four **bases**: adenine (A), thymine (T), cytosine (C) and guanine (G).
- The sugar and phosphate groups alternate, forming the **sugar–phosphate backbone**. The phosphate links C3 of one sugar to C5 of the next.
- There are two strands, wound as a **double helix**. They are held together by **hydrogen bonds** between **complementary base pairs**: **A–T (2 hydrogen bonds)** and **C–G (3 hydrogen bonds)**.
# The anticancer drug cisplatin
- Cisplatin, **cis-[Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂]**, is a square planar platinum(II) complex.
- In the cell, its two Cl⁻ ligands are replaced by **nitrogen atoms of guanine bases** on DNA (a ligand substitution reaction). This forms a cross-link that distorts the DNA and **prevents replication**, so the cancer cell cannot divide.
- **Adverse effects:** cisplatin also affects healthy cells that divide rapidly, such as hair follicles, causing hair loss. It can also damage the kidneys. So the dose is carefully controlled.
:::ex Why the cis isomer
Q: Why is the trans isomer of [Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂] much less effective?
1. In the cis isomer, the two Cl ligands are next to each other (at 90°), so the Pt can bond to two neighbouring guanines on the same strand.
2. In the trans isomer, the leaving groups are on opposite sides (at 180°) and cannot bridge two neighbouring bases.
A: Only the cis shape allows the DNA cross-link to form.
:::
:::warn
- Drawing a zwitterion as H₂N–CHR–COO⁻ with no charge on N. A zwitterion has **both** charges.
- Saying that the secondary structure is held by bonds between side chains. That is the **tertiary** structure; secondary structure involves hydrogen bonds in the backbone.
- Pairing A with G, or giving C–G only 2 hydrogen bonds.
:::
:::tip
- For cisplatin, name the bond formed: a **co-ordinate bond from N of guanine to Pt**, replacing Cl⁻.
- For TLC questions, always say that amino acids are colourless and must be located with ninhydrin or UV light.
:::
:::sum
- Amino acids are zwitterions; their form depends on pH.
- Proteins are held by peptide links (primary structure), H-bonds (secondary) and H-bonds, ionic attractions and S–S bridges (tertiary). Hydrolysis and TLC identify the amino acids.
- Enzymes are stereospecific; inhibitors block the active site.
- DNA is a phosphate/deoxyribose backbone with A–T and C–G hydrogen-bonded base pairs.
- Cisplatin binds guanine N atoms and stops replication, but it also has side effects.
:::
