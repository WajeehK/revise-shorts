@intro Waves transfer energy without transferring matter. This topic covers wave properties, the electromagnetic spectrum, polarisation, refraction, interference and stationary waves.
# Describing waves
!fig p-wave Displacement–distance graph of a progressive wave
:::def
**Displacement:** the distance of a point from its equilibrium position.
**Amplitude (A):** the maximum displacement.
**Wavelength (λ):** the minimum distance between two points oscillating in phase.
**Period (T):** the time for one complete oscillation; f = 1/T.
**Phase difference:** how far one oscillation is ahead of another, as a fraction of a cycle, measured in degrees or radians.
**Intensity:** power per unit area, I = P/A, in W m⁻². Intensity is proportional to amplitude²: I ∝ A².
:::
- **Wave equation:** v = fλ.
- **Transverse** waves oscillate perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer (light, waves on strings). **Longitudinal** waves oscillate parallel to it, with compressions and rarefactions (sound).
- **Polarisation** confines the oscillations to a single plane. Only **transverse** waves can be polarised. Examples are Polaroid filters and the polarisation of microwaves by a metal grille.
- Waves undergo **reflection**, **refraction** and **diffraction**. Diffraction is greatest when the gap is about the same size as the wavelength.
:::ex Wave speed
Q: A sound wave has a frequency of 680 Hz and a wavelength of 0.50 m. Find its speed, and the phase difference between two points 0.125 m apart along the wave.
1. v = fλ = 680 × 0.50 = 340 m s⁻¹.
2. 0.125 m is λ/4, so the phase difference is 90° (π/2 rad).
A: 340 m s⁻¹ and 90°
:::
# Electromagnetic waves and refraction
- All EM waves travel at c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹ in a vacuum. In order of decreasing wavelength: radio, microwave, infrared, visible (about 400–700 nm), ultraviolet, X-rays and γ-rays.
:::eq
$$n=\frac{c}{v}\qquad n_1\sin\theta_1=n_2\sin\theta_2\qquad \sin C=\frac1n$$
:::
- **Total internal reflection** happens when light travels from a higher to a lower refractive index **and** the angle of incidence is greater than the critical angle C. It is used in optical fibres.
# Superposition and interference
- **Principle of superposition:** when waves meet, the resultant displacement is the sum of the individual displacements.
- **Coherent** sources have a constant phase difference and the same frequency.
- **Constructive interference** occurs where the path difference is nλ. **Destructive interference** occurs where it is (n + ½)λ.
!fig p-interference Two-source interference pattern
:::eq
$$\lambda=\frac{ax}{D}\ \ (\text{Young's double slits})\qquad d\sin\theta=n\lambda\ \ (\text{diffraction grating})$$
:::
:::ex Diffraction grating
Q: A grating with 300 lines per mm is lit with light of wavelength 600 nm. Find the angle of the 2nd-order maximum, and the highest order visible.
1. d = 1/300000 = 3.33 × 10⁻⁶ m.
2. sin θ = 2 × 600 × 10⁻⁹ / 3.33 × 10⁻⁶ = 0.36, so θ = 21°.
3. The maximum order is when sin θ ≤ 1: n ≤ d/λ = 5.6, so n = 5.
A: 21°, and the highest order is 5.
:::
# Stationary waves
!fig p-stationary Stationary-wave harmonics on a string
- A stationary wave forms when two waves of the same frequency travelling in **opposite directions** superpose, for example a wave and its reflection.
- **Nodes** have zero amplitude; **antinodes** have maximum amplitude. Adjacent nodes are λ/2 apart.
- Stationary waves **store** energy; they do not transfer it. All points between adjacent nodes are in phase.
| System | Fundamental (1st harmonic) |
|---|---|
| string fixed at both ends | L = λ/2 |
| tube closed at one end | L = λ/4; only odd harmonics occur |
| tube open at both ends | L = λ/2 |
:::prac PAG 5: speed of sound using a resonance tube
- Hold a tuning fork over a tube in water. Adjust the length of the air column until the first resonance (the loudest sound) is heard. Then L + end correction = λ/4.
- Find the next resonance at 3λ/4. The difference in length is λ/2, so the end correction cancels. Then v = fλ.
:::
:::prac Wave practicals
- **Ripple tank.** Techniques and procedures used to demonstrate wave effects using a ripple tank: reflection off a barrier, refraction over a shallow region, diffraction through a gap and interference from two dippers. A strobe "freezes" the pattern.
- **Polarisation.** Observe polarising effects using microwaves (rotate the receiver and the signal falls to zero at 90°) and light (two Polaroid filters).
- **Refraction and TIR.** Use ray boxes with transparent rectangular and semi-circular blocks to measure angles of incidence and refraction, and find the critical angle.
:::
- The **fundamental mode** of vibration (1st harmonic) is the simplest stationary wave with one loop. Higher modes of vibration are harmonics: for a string fixed at both ends, the nth harmonic has frequency nf₀.
:::warn
- Saying that sound can be polarised. It is longitudinal, so it cannot.
- Using the slit width instead of the slit separation in λ = ax/D.
- Confusing node spacing (λ/2) with wavelength.
:::
:::tip
- For a grating, convert "lines per mm" to d in metres before doing anything else.
:::
:::sum
- v = fλ; I = P/A ∝ A²; only transverse waves can be polarised.
- n = c/v; Snell's law; TIR when θ > C.
- Interference: λ = ax/D and d sin θ = nλ.
- Stationary waves have nodes λ/2 apart and harmonics set by the boundary conditions.
:::
