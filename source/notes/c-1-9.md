@intro Rate equations show exactly how the rate depends on each concentration. This topic covers orders, the rate constant and its units, the Arrhenius equation, finding orders from experiments and graphs, and linking the rate equation to the rate-determining step.
# Rate equations and orders
$$\text{rate}=k[\text{A}]^m[\text{B}]^n$$
:::def
**Order with respect to a reactant:** the power of its concentration in the rate equation (0, 1 or 2 at A-level).
**Overall order:** the sum of the individual orders, m + n.
**Rate constant, k:** the constant of proportionality in the rate equation. It is fixed at a given temperature.
:::
- Orders can only be found by **experiment**. They are not taken from the balanced equation.
- **Zero order:** changing the concentration has no effect on the rate. **First order:** doubling the concentration doubles the rate. **Second order:** doubling the concentration ×4 the rate.
:::eq
| Overall order | Units of k |
|---|---|
| 0 | mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹ |
| 1 | s⁻¹ |
| 2 | mol⁻¹ dm³ s⁻¹ |
| 3 | mol⁻² dm⁶ s⁻¹ |
:::
:::ex Finding orders from initial rates
Q: For A + B → products. Experiment 1: [A] = 0.10, [B] = 0.10, rate = 2.0 × 10⁻⁴. Experiment 2: [A] = 0.20, [B] = 0.10, rate = 8.0 × 10⁻⁴. Experiment 3: [A] = 0.10, [B] = 0.30, rate = 6.0 × 10⁻⁴ (concentrations in mol dm⁻³, rates in mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹). Find the rate equation and k.
1. From 1 to 2, [A] ×2 and the rate ×4, so the order in A is 2.
2. From 1 to 3, [B] ×3 and the rate ×3, so the order in B is 1.
3. The rate equation is rate = k[A]²[B]. Using experiment 1: $k=\frac{2.0\times10^{-4}}{(0.10)^2(0.10)}=0.20$.
A: rate = k[A]²[B]; k = 0.20 mol⁻² dm⁶ s⁻¹
:::
# Temperature and the Arrhenius equation
$$k=Ae^{-E_a/RT}\qquad \ln k=-\frac{E_a}{R}\cdot\frac1T+\ln A$$
- A rise in temperature increases **k**, so the rate increases. Concentrations are unchanged.
- A plot of **ln k against 1/T** is a straight line with gradient $-E_a/R$ and intercept $\ln A$. Use $R$ = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ and $T$ in K.
:::ex Activation energy from a graph
Q: A plot of ln k against 1/T has gradient −6500 K. Calculate Eₐ.
1. Gradient = −Eₐ/R, so Eₐ = 6500 × 8.31.
2. That is 54 015 J mol⁻¹.
A: Eₐ ≈ 54 kJ mol⁻¹
:::
# Finding orders from graphs
!fig c-rate-orders Concentration–time and rate–concentration graphs for orders 0, 1 and 2
- **Concentration–time graph.** Zero order gives a straight line with a constant gradient. First order gives a curve with a **constant half-life**. Second order gives a curve whose half-life increases.
- **Rate–concentration graph.** Zero order is a horizontal line. First order is a straight line through the origin. Second order is a curve (a parabola). A plot of rate against [A]² is a straight line for second order.
- The rate at a given time is the **gradient of the tangent** to the concentration–time curve.
- For first order, $k=\frac{\ln 2}{t_{1/2}}$.
!fig c-half-life Constant half-life for a first-order reaction
:::prac Required practical 7: measuring rate
- **Initial rate (clock) method.** In the iodine clock, a fixed small amount of thiosulfate is added, so the blue-black colour appears when a fixed amount of iodine has formed. The initial rate is proportional to 1/time. Repeat with different starting concentrations of one reactant at a time.
- **Continuous monitoring.** Follow one reaction over time, for example by colorimetry, gas volume or titrating samples that have been quenched. Plot concentration against time and draw tangents.
:::
# Rate-determining step
:::def
**Rate-determining step (RDS):** the slowest step in a mechanism. It controls the overall rate.
:::
- Species that appear in the rate equation, and in the right numbers, are involved in the RDS or in steps before it. A zero-order reactant appears only **after** the RDS.
- Example: for 2-bromo-2-methylpropane + OH⁻, rate = k[(CH₃)₃CBr]. So the RDS involves only the halogenoalkane, which ionises to a carbocation first; OH⁻ attacks afterwards.
:::warn
- Taking the orders from the coefficients in the balanced equation.
- Forgetting to convert °C to K, or Eₐ from J to kJ.
- Saying that a catalyst or a higher temperature changes the orders. Temperature changes k, not the orders.
:::
:::tip
- In initial-rate tables, compare two experiments where only one concentration changes. If no such pair exists, use one known order to find the other.
- Always give units for k. Derive them by rearranging k = rate ÷ (concentration terms).
:::
:::sum
- rate = k[A]^m[B]^n, with orders from experiment only.
- The units of k depend on the overall order. k increases with temperature: ln k = −Eₐ/RT + ln A.
- Graph shapes identify the order. First order has a constant half-life.
- The rate equation shows which species are in the rate-determining step.
:::
