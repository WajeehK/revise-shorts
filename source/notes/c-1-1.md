@intro Everything in chemistry comes back to atoms: how many protons, neutrons and electrons they have, and how the electrons are arranged. This topic covers the particles in an atom, isotopes and the time-of-flight mass spectrometer, and how ionisation energies give evidence for shells and sub-shells.
# Fundamental particles
Our model of the atom has changed over time as new evidence appeared. Dalton pictured solid spheres. Thomson's discovery of the electron led to the "plum pudding" model. Rutherford's gold-foil experiment showed a tiny, dense, positive nucleus. Bohr then put the electrons in fixed energy levels. The modern model uses orbitals. Each model was accepted because it explained the experimental results of its time better than the one before.
| Particle | Relative mass | Relative charge | Where it is found |
|---|---|---|---|
| proton | 1 | +1 | nucleus |
| neutron | 1 | 0 | nucleus |
| electron | 1/1840 (about 0.0005) | −1 | shells (orbitals) around the nucleus |
- The nucleus contains almost all the mass but takes up only a tiny fraction of the volume of the atom.
- A neutral atom has equal numbers of protons and electrons.
# Mass number and isotopes
:::def
**Atomic (proton) number, Z:** the number of protons in the nucleus. It defines the element.
**Mass number, A:** the total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus.
**Isotopes:** atoms of the same element with the same number of protons but different numbers of neutrons.
:::
- Number of neutrons $= A - Z$. For an ion, electrons $= Z - \text{charge}$. So $^{32}_{16}\text{S}^{2-}$ has 16 protons, 16 neutrons and 18 electrons.
- Isotopes have the **same chemical properties** because they have the same electron configuration. They have slightly different **physical properties**, such as density and rate of diffusion, because their masses differ.
# The time-of-flight (TOF) mass spectrometer
A TOF mass spectrometer measures the mass-to-charge ratio ($m/z$) of ions. It works in four stages.
1. **Ionisation.** In **electrospray ionisation**, the sample is dissolved in a volatile solvent and forced through a fine, high-voltage needle. Each molecule gains a proton: $\text{X(g)} + \text{H}^+ \rightarrow \text{XH}^+\text{(g)}$. This is used for larger molecules because they don't break up. In **electron impact**, high-energy electrons from an electron gun knock an electron off each particle: $\text{X(g)} \rightarrow \text{X}^+\text{(g)} + \text{e}^-$. This is used for elements and small molecules, and the molecules often fragment.
2. **Acceleration.** An electric field accelerates the positive ions so that they all have the **same kinetic energy**.
3. **Ion drift.** The ions pass through a flight tube with no field. Because $E_k=\frac12mv^2$ is the same for all of them, lighter ions travel faster and reach the detector first.
4. **Detection.** Each ion picks up an electron at the detector. This produces a current, and the current is proportional to the number of ions (the abundance).
$$t = d\sqrt{\frac{m}{2E_k}}\qquad\text{(time of flight is proportional to }\sqrt{m}\text{)}$$
!fig c-mass-spec Mass spectrum of chlorine gas: Cl₂⁺ peaks at m/z 70, 72 and 74 in the ratio 9 : 6 : 1
- **Uses.** The pattern of isotopes identifies an element. For a molecular substance, the peak with the largest $m/z$ (the molecular ion, M⁺, or $[\text{M+H}]^+$ in electrospray) gives the relative molecular mass. With electrospray you subtract 1 for the added proton.
:::eq
$$A_r=\frac{\sum(\text{isotopic mass}\times\text{abundance})}{\sum\text{abundance}}$$
:::
:::ex Relative atomic mass from a spectrum
Q: A sample of magnesium gives peaks at $m/z$ 24 (78.6%), 25 (10.1%) and 26 (11.3%). Calculate $A_r$ to 1 decimal place.
1. Multiply each mass by its abundance: $24\times78.6=1886.4$, $25\times10.1=252.5$, $26\times11.3=293.8$.
2. Add them: $1886.4+252.5+293.8=2432.7$.
3. Divide by the total abundance (100): $2432.7/100=24.327$.
A: $A_r = 24.3$
:::
:::ex Working out abundances
Q: Boron has isotopes ¹⁰B and ¹¹B, and its $A_r$ is 10.8. Find the percentage of ¹⁰B.
1. Let the percentage of ¹⁰B be $x$, so ¹¹B is $100-x$.
2. Then $\frac{10x+11(100-x)}{100}=10.8$, which gives $1100-x=1080$.
3. So $x=20$.
A: 20% ¹⁰B and 80% ¹¹B.
:::
# Electron configuration
Electrons occupy **shells** (main energy levels, $n=1,2,3\dots$), which are split into **sub-shells** (s, p, d). Each sub-shell is made of **orbitals**. Every orbital holds up to two electrons with opposite spins.
| Sub-shell | Orbitals | Maximum electrons |
|---|---|---|
| s | 1 | 2 |
| p | 3 | 6 |
| d | 5 | 10 |
!fig c-orbitals Shapes of s and p orbitals
- **Filling order (up to Z = 36):** 1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d 4p. The 4s sub-shell fills before 3d because it is slightly lower in energy.
- Orbitals in the same sub-shell fill singly first, then pair up.
- **Exceptions:** Cr is [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹ and Cu is [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹, because a half-full or full d sub-shell is more stable.
- **Ions of transition metals** lose their **4s electrons first**: Fe is [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s², so Fe³⁺ is [Ar] 3d⁵.
- The block an element sits in (s, p, d) tells you the sub-shell of its highest-energy electron.
# Ionisation energies
:::def
**First ionisation energy:** the energy needed to remove one electron from each atom in one mole of gaseous atoms, forming one mole of gaseous 1+ ions.
:::
$$\text{Na(g)}\rightarrow\text{Na}^+\text{(g)}+\text{e}^-\qquad\text{(1st IE)}$$
$$\text{Na}^+\text{(g)}\rightarrow\text{Na}^{2+}\text{(g)}+\text{e}^-\qquad\text{(2nd IE)}$$
Ionisation energy depends on three things: **nuclear charge** (the number of protons), **distance** of the electron from the nucleus, and **shielding** by inner electrons.
- **Successive ionisation energies** always increase, because each electron is removed from an increasingly positive ion. A **big jump** shows that the next electron comes from a shell closer to the nucleus. For example, Al has jumps after the 3rd and the 11th electrons, which is evidence for the 2, 8, 3 arrangement.
- **Down Group 2 (Be–Ba)**, the first IE decreases. The outer electron is in a higher shell, further from the nucleus and more shielded. This outweighs the increase in nuclear charge.
- **Across Period 3 (Na–Ar)**, the first IE generally increases. Nuclear charge increases while shielding stays similar, so the atomic radius gets smaller.
!fig c-ie-period3 First ionisation energies across Period 3 with the dips at Al and S
- **Dip at Al.** Al's outer electron is in a 3p orbital, which is higher in energy than 3s and slightly shielded by the 3s electrons. This is evidence for **sub-shells**.
- **Dip at S.** S has a pair of electrons in one 3p orbital. They repel each other, so one is easier to remove. This is evidence for **electron pairing** in orbitals.
:::ex Reading successive ionisation energies
Q: The first five ionisation energies of element X (kJ mol⁻¹) are 578, 1817, 2745, 11 577 and 14 842. Which group is X in?
1. Look for the large jump. Between the 3rd (2745) and 4th (11 577) IE the value roughly quadruples.
2. The 4th electron is therefore removed from an inner shell, so X has 3 outer electrons.
A: Group 13 (X is aluminium).
:::
:::warn
- Writing ionisation equations without state symbols, or with an electron *added* on the left. It is always X(g) → X⁺(g) + e⁻.
- Saying 4s electrons are lost *after* 3d when ions form. They are lost **first**.
- Explaining isotopes as having "different numbers of protons". The protons are the same; the neutrons differ.
- In TOF, saying the ions are "deflected" by a magnetic field. That is an older instrument; TOF separates ions by time of flight.
:::
:::tip
- For a trend in IE, always mention nuclear charge, distance (or shells) and shielding. Then say which effect wins.
- Learn the Cr and Cu exceptions and the Period 3 dips at Al (3p) and S (pairing). They are favourite 2-mark questions.
:::
:::sum
- Protons (+1, mass 1) and neutrons (0, mass 1) are in the nucleus; electrons (−1, mass ≈ 1/1840) are around it.
- Isotopes have the same Z and different A, so they have the same chemistry.
- TOF mass spectrometry: ionise, accelerate (same $E_k$), drift (lighter ions are faster), detect. It gives isotopic masses, abundances and $M_r$.
- Electron configurations use 1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d. Cr and Cu are exceptions, and ions lose 4s first.
- Ionisation energy trends are explained by nuclear charge, distance and shielding. The Period 3 dips at Al and S are evidence for sub-shells and pairing.
:::
